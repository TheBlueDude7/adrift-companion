## Chapter 0: The Stars, 2274 AD

Lore and Information

Humanity did not meet the enemy on Earth.

It met them far from home, in the cold dark beyond the outer planets, where gravity wells are weak and mistakes are final.

They did not speak. They did not negotiate. They did not retreat. When human ships vanished along the frontier, there were no survivors and no wreckage, only empty orbits and sensor ghosts.

This unknown enemy, dubbed the “Azal”, laid waste to many human colonies, burning their way closer and closer to Earth. Their warships flew in fleets that seemed to blot out the stars. By the time humanity understood it was an invasion, it was already too late to stop cleanly.

So the last of humanity’s fleet gathered at the edge of the Solar System. Everything that could still fly. Everything that could still fire. Crews drawn from colonies, stations, and half-finished training programs. The Azal had bore a line straight to Earth, as if aiming straight for the heart itself, once they deciphered its location. Orders rewritten daily as intelligence failed to keep pace with reality. The battle that followed was chaos.

You remember fragments. Flurries of lasers flying through space. Comms filled with overlapping voices and brutal static. Sensor officers calling out readings that made no sense. Then a flash of bright white.

---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

You wake to alarms screaming in broken, overlapping tones.

The air tastes thin and metallic. Each breath takes effort, not because you’re panicking, but because there simply isn’t enough oxygen left to waste. Emergency lights flicker, casting long, uneven shadows across torn bulkheads and drifting debris.

When you try to move, pain answers first.

Your body feels heavy, uncooperative. Blood drips from your open wounds, and sharp pain radiates through your shattered bones. Every motion reminds you how close you came to not waking up at all.

Peering through your closest viewport to space, a single, bright white star flickers in the distance, its light pulsing unevenly, as if the system itself is unstable. Around you drifts a graveyard: shattered hulls, fractured spines of ships, debris fields stretching farther than your sensors can confidently track. Human designs. And an even more horrifying revelation - there are no stars. Not a single one in sight, except for the center of this system.

Your ship is barely holding together. Power is failing. Supplies are limited. There are no friendly signals. No command channels. No response to your hails. You don’t know who won the battle.

You don’t know how you ended up here. You aren’t aware if any of your crew has survived. And most certainly, you have no clue how to get home.

If there is one left.

### Technology

#### What Humanity Has Mastered

- Fusion power
- Sustained interstellar travel
- Artificial gravity
- Advanced automation
- Long-duration life support
- Cybernetic augmentation (limited, invasive, and expensive)
- Gene therapies

#### What Humanity Has Not Solved

- Faster-than-light communication without delay
- Instant healing or regeneration
- True artificial intelligence autonomy (most systems are narrow, task-bound)
- Cheap or universal cybernetics
- Immunity to radiation, disease, or psychological breakdown
- Infinite energy or matter

### Medical Reality

#### First Aid

- Can stabilize trauma
- Can stop bleeding
- Can prevent shock
- Can slow infection
- Cannot restore lost tissue
- Cannot undo organ damage

#### Surgery

- Requires time, tools, power, and sterile conditions
- Can repair organs, bones, and internal damage
- Can implant cybernetics
- Carries risk of complications
- Consumes medical supplies

#### Cybernetics

Cybernetics exist, but they are:

- Purpose-built
- Difficult to install
- Physically taxing

Common cybernetics:

- Prosthetic limbs
- Reinforced joints
- Sensory replacements (eyes, cochlear implants)
- Neural interface ports

There are many uncommon cybernetics too, especially banned-black market versions that have a heavier toll but do much, much more damage.

Limitations:

- Require maintenance
- Draw power or metabolic resources
- Increase stress and complication risk
- Can malfunction under radiation or EM interference

## Chapter 1: Still Breathing

Character Building

Welcome to Adrift.

Adrift takes place after the end of a brutal battle humanity was not prepared to lose. You do not begin this game as a hero, a chosen one, or a legend. You begin as a survivor, one who is injured, exhausted, and cut off from everything that once made the universe feel predictable.

Your character is one of the few humans(or at least, that's what you think), left alive in a dead system, aboard a ship that should not still be functioning. Supplies are scarce. The environment is hostile. Help is not coming. At least, not yet.

This chapter defines who your character was before everything went wrong, and what they are capable of now.

Base for Characters

Every character in Adrift starts with base stats that they can use.

Each human starts with:

- 8 hp,
- 3 max energy,
- 1 willpower,
- And 1 in every base attribute.
- 6 attribute points to spend.
- 45 Skill XP to spend.

Hp, Energy, and Willpower will be discussed in a later chapter. Your attributes and skill points will increase based on what you do in Adrift. You can learn new things and train your body, as there is only so much to do aboard a drifting derelict.

#### Core Attributes

Every character in Adrift is defined by four core attributes. These represent your character’s fundamental strengths - the things that remain when exhaustion, fear, and injury strip everything else away.

- Strength: Physical power, endurance under load, and the ability to force the world to move when it refuses.
- Every point of STR above 1 increases your max health by 1.
- Constitution: Health, energy, and resistance to injury, illness, and long-term strain(including stress).
- Every point of CON above 1 increases your max health by 2.
- Every 2 points of CON above 1 increases your max energy by 1.
- Every 3 points of CON above 1 increases your max willpower by 1.
- CON cannot retroactively heal damage when increased. That is to say, going from 3 -> 4 CON does not mean you gain the hp immediately.
- Intelligence: Technical knowledge, problem-solving, and the ability to turn broken systems into working ones.
- Wits: Awareness, instinct, perception, and the ability to react before disaster strikes.

### Skills

Skills represent training, experience, and practiced behavior. They determine how effectively your character performs tasks and how much energy those tasks consume.

Unlike attributes, skills improve frequently through play. Survival teaches quickly. All skills start at level 0.

#### Skill Checks

When your character attempts an action where the outcome is uncertain, the GM may call for a skill roll. This usually involves crafting, treatment, and anything pertaining to a skill in Adrift.

To make a skill roll:

- Roll a number of six-sided dice (d6) equal to: Relevant Attribute + Skill Level
- Each die that rolls a 6 counts as a success.
- One success is enough to succeed.
- Additional successes generate stunts — situational advantages, increased efficiency, reduced energy cost, or other benefits determined by the GM.

If no dice show a 6, the action fails — often creating new complications rather than simply stopping progress, or worse.

#### Skill List

STRENGTH-Based Skills

- Close Combat - Bludgeoning, swords, and more.
- Light Firearms – Pistols, rifles, and personal-scale ranged weapons
- Heavy Weaponry – Mounted weapons, explosives, and heavy ordinance
- Acrobatics – Balance, zero-G movement, and physical agility

INT-Based Skills

- First Aid – Emergency treatment and stabilizing injuries
- Surgery – Complex medical procedures and invasive care
- Botany – Growing food and controlled cultivation
- Biology - Identifying and understanding viable organisms.
- Piloting – Operating spacecraft and small craft under pressure
- Navigation – Charting courses, interpreting signals, and spatial orientation
- Mechanical Technician – Repairing machinery, tools, and mechanical systems
- Engineering – Power systems, structural integrity, and advanced repairs

WIT-Based Skills

- Perception – Awareness, detection, and reading dangerous environments
- Stealth – Avoiding detection and moving unseen
- Scavenging – Finding usable materials in wreckage and hostile environments
- Cooking – Preparing food and safe water under limited conditions, stretching rations, and avoiding contamination.
- Entertainer – Stress reduction and persuasion related-rolls

#### Research Rolls Wits

Research is the one place a skill does not roll its own attribute. Analysis, extraction, and the research Projects roll:

Wits + the relevant knowledge skill

Knowing what a thing is comes out of the textbook. Noticing what matters about it does not. A researcher still needs Biology or Engineering to do the work, but it is instinct that decides whether they see the pattern in front of them.

This covers Analyze Known Biomass, Analyze Unknown Biomass, Analyze Technical Data, Extract Research Trait, Cybernetic Research, and Xenoflora Cultivation. Every other Task rolls the attribute its skill is listed under.

## Stunts

Rolling more than one 6 on any skill-based roll generates a stunt. Detailed Stunt descriptions can be found in the Adrift compendium. For any 6 above one, choose any of the following stunts for the related skills.

### Experience & Advancement

Advancement is handled separately for skills and attributes, with skills improving far more often. Attributes can rise - but only in specific circumstances the GM prescribes. When a character spends time deliberately practicing a skill in a safe or controlled environment, they gain 1 XP in that skill. Skills range from Level 0 to Level 10. XP can also be awarded in other circumstances.

Reaching level 10 in a skill adds +1 to the original attribute its tied to.

### XP Costs to Increase Skill Levels

Increasing a skill requires accumulating XP equal to the next level’s cost.

### Training Resources

<!-- updated: training-materials -->

A Train Skill segment spends one unit of a resource from the hold and returns 1 XP. Which resource depends on the skill and on the level being trained **toward**, not the level already held: a common material for levels 1–3, a refined one for 4–7, and a rare one for 8–10. The resource is used whether or not the session earns its XP.

Training only ever consumes raw materials and basic supplies. It never consumes finished equipment — no Medkits, Power Cells, Toolkits, or cooked meals. This matters because the XP ladder compounds: taking one skill from 0 to 10 is 6 units of its common material, 22 of its refined one, and 27 of its rare one. A finished item in any column would quietly price mastery at twenty-seven Glitterworld Medkits.

If a training resource does carry charges, one segment gives up a single charge rather than the whole unit.

## Backgrounds

A Background represents the training, profession, and life a character possessed before becoming Adrift.

### Choosing a Background

Each character chooses one Background during character creation.

A Background provides:

- Attribute Bonuses.
- Skill Bonuses.
- One fixed Signature Trait.
- One chosen Background Specialty.

Background abilities cannot normally be gained through:

- Skill advancement.
- Research.
- Crafting.
- Cybernetics.
- Biological augmentations.
- Mech modules.
- Training from another character.

A Background ability may only be copied when a rule explicitly says that it grants a Background ability.

Background abilities continue functioning while operating a mech unless the ability requires a bodily action the mech physically prevents.

Two instances of the same named Background effect do not stack.

Both a Signature Trait and a chosen Specialty count as Background abilities. Effects such as Identity Erosion disable both.

## Chapter 2: Counting the Hours

### Survival Mechanics

Survival in Adrift is not measured in minutes.

It is measured in days.

Every day is a limited set of decisions made with incomplete information. The crew cannot repair every system, treat every Injury, prepare every resource, and explore every signal at once. They must decide what matters now, assign the right people, and preserve enough strength to respond when circumstances change.

### The Core Procedure

Aboard the ship, play advances through four daily segments: Morning, Afternoon, Evening, and Night. The crew plans and resolves one segment at a time. This prevents a single event from invalidating an entire day and gives the players a chance to adapt as conditions change.

1. Begin the segment: resolve effects scheduled for that segment.

2. Assess the ship: the GM states all known threats, shortages, damaged systems, deadlines, and available opportunities.

3. Plan together: the crew discusses priorities and assigns one task to each available character.

4. Classify each task as Routine, Skilled, or Crisis.

5. Pay Energy and commit required materials.

6. Resolve Routine tasks automatically, then resolve Skilled and Crisis tasks.

7. Resolve any event, interruption, or consequence caused during the segment.

8. Advance clocks and Projects, then begin the next segment.

At the end of Night, complete the End of Day procedure.

### Tasks

A task is a meaningful use of one day segment. Unless a rule says otherwise, each character may perform one task per segment. Eating, drinking, brief conversation, and moving normally around a secure ship do not consume a task.

#### Routine Tasks

A Routine task succeeds automatically when all listed requirements are met. The character still spends the task, Energy, materials, charges, and other listed costs.

- The character meets or exceeds the task’s Routine Threshold.
- All required tools and facilities are present and functional.
- The correct materials are available.
- The task is performed at its normal pace.
- The environment is secure and no immediate threat interferes.

Meeting a Routine Threshold does not forbid anyone else from trying. A character below the threshold may attempt the same work as a Skilled task.

<!-- updated: routine-benefits, mastery -->

#### Routine Benefits

A character who meets a task's Routine Threshold qualifies for Routine Benefits:

- Practiced (3+ levels above the threshold): the task costs 1 less Energy. This applies to any Routine task, not only crafting.
- Expert (6+ levels above): the task earns its Strong Result without rolling.
- Project Efficiency (4+ levels above): +1 Progress per Routine segment, for 2 in total.

<!-- updated: labor, labor-projects -->

#### Labor

Some work aboard needs no training, only a back. These Tasks list a Labor Threshold in place of a skill, and your Strength stands in for the Skill Level.

- A character whose Strength meets the Labor Threshold performs the Task as Routine.
- Below it, the Task is Skilled and rolls flat Strength: the attribute alone, with no Skill Level to add.
- Injuries that cost you Strength rolls cost you Labor too.
- An Assist on a Labor Task lends the helper's Strength instead of a Skill Level, to the usual maximum of +4d6.

Strength rises far more slowly than a skill and stops far lower, so Labor keeps its own Routine Benefits:

| Routine Benefit | Skill | Labor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Practiced (1 less Energy) | 3 above | 1 above |
| Expert (Strong Result without rolling) | 6 above | 2 above |
| Project Efficiency (2 Progress) | 4 above | 2 above |

##### Lending a Back

Labor is also how an untrained character becomes useful on a Ship Project. On any Project that builds, moves, or repairs something physical, a character may Assist a contributor on **Strength 3** in place of the Project's own skill. They are carrying plate, holding things steady, and taking the weight.

A successful Labor Assist adds 1 Progress and takes one of the Project's contributor slots, exactly like any other Assist. The one restriction is the point of it:

> A Labor Assist only adds Progress if the contributor it helps is working the Project as Routine — that is, if they meet the Project's own Routine Threshold.

Muscle multiplies trained work. It never replaces it. A crew of strong backs with nobody who can build still cannot build, and helping someone who is themselves struggling through a Skilled roll achieves nothing but a sore shoulder.

This does not apply to Projects where there is nothing to lift: Stress Recovery, Affliction Treatment, Cybernetic Research, and Xenoflora Cultivation.

## Skilled Tasks

A Skilled Task represents work whose outcome is genuinely uncertain but whose failure does not immediately create catastrophe.

Roll the listed:

Attribute + Skill Level

Each die showing a 6 is a success.

#### Failure

If no dice show a 6, the Task fails.

Unless the Task states otherwise:

- No result or Progress is produced.
- The character's Energy is spent.
- The segment is spent.

#### Success

If at least one 6 is rolled, the Task succeeds. The character receives the Task's listed normal result.

#### Strong Success

If two or more 6s are rolled, the character scores a Strong Success.

<!-- updated: strong-results -->

A Strong Success only provides an additional effect when the Task, item, ability, or subsystem lists a Strong Result. If no Strong Result is listed, additional successes do not change the outcome. Outside combat, the Compendium's skill stunt lists are each skill's default Strong Results.

## Strong Successes

A Strong Success represents an unusually effective result where degrees of success actually matter.

#### Analyze Unknown Biomass

Success: Identify the organism and its most important known property.

Strong Success: Also identify a viable Research Trait or important biological weakness.

#### Scan Nearby Space

Success: Reveal basic information about the target area.

Strong Success: Also reveal a concealed signal, hazard, or secondary point of interest.

#### Crisis Tasks

A Crisis task is performed under immediate danger or has consequences too severe to become automatic. Crisis tasks always require a roll, even for experts.

- Surgery on a dying patient.
- Sealing an active breach during decompression.
- Restarting a reactor during overload.
- Repairing or crafting while under attack.
- Piloting through an unscanned gravitational hazard.
- Handling unknown alien biology without containment.

Before a Crisis roll, the GM states what failure will cause.

## Combat Exception: Stunts

Combat uses its own rules for additional successes.

When a combat rule allows Stunts, additional successes may be spent on the listed tactical effects described in the combat chapter.

Combat Stunts are intentionally more flexible because positioning, damage, control, and immediate tactical opportunities change from moment to moment. Unless a combat rule specifically refers to a Strong Success, use the combat Stunt rules instead.

<!-- updated: strong-results -->

Outside combat, additional successes do not create generic Stunts; they only matter when a task lists a Strong Result. Generic Stunts are for combat.

#### Task Modifiers

Use penalties only when circumstances meaningfully impair the attempt. Do not apply a penalty merely because a character is below a Routine Threshold; being below the threshold is already the reason a roll is required.

| Circumstance | Modifier |
| --- | --- |
| Minor complication: cramped access, poor lighting, distracting noise | -1d6 |
| Serious complication: damaged tools, substitute materials, impaired facility | -2d6 |
| Severe complication: active decompression, combat, violent motion, extreme radiation | -3d6 |
| Exceptional preparation beyond normal requirements | +1d6 to +3d6 |

Modifiers normally stack to a maximum of -6d6 or +6d6. A task that has become impossible because a required component or capability is completely absent cannot be attempted until the crew changes the situation.

#### Assistance

One character may spend a task assisting another character. The assistant must have at least 1 level in the relevant skill, the Strength to lend a back on physical Project work, or a clearly useful method of helping.

<!-- updated: assist, labor-projects -->

- Assisting costs 1 Energy.
- The assistant resolves their Assist against the helped task's skill, Routine Threshold 1, and only a successful Assist does anything.
- On a Skilled or Crisis task, a successful Assist adds the assistant's Skill Level as bonus dice, to a maximum of +4d6.
- Only one assistant adds dice to a task: the highest Skill Level among those who succeeded.
- On a Project, a successful Assist adds 1 Progress if the assistant meets the Project's Routine Threshold, and takes one of its contributor slots.
- On a Project that builds, moves, or repairs something physical, an assistant may qualify on Strength 3 instead, provided the contributor they help is working it as Routine. See Lending a Back.
- Assistance never turns a Crisis task into a Routine task.
- Resolve Assists before the task they help, so the helped character knows their pool.

#### Crop Varieties

<!-- updated: crop-varieties -->

Hydroponics beds grow one thing at a time, and the crew chooses what. Every variety takes the same three days, the same tending, and the same Botany threshold to sow. Only the harvest differs, and the rarer the produce the less of it a crop gives.

| Variety | Seeded | Operational | Expanded | Self-Sustaining | Harvest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Food | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 | Raw Food, the galley’s bulk calories. |
| Chemical | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | Chemical Mixture, the stock for Special Brews, Painkillers, and Psychiatric Medication. |
| Fiber | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | Scrapped Biomass, a Tier II biological material. |

Giving the beds over to anything but Food means the crew stops growing their own food, which is the whole of the choice.

Expanded and Self-Sustaining beds replant themselves and never stand empty, so a switch has to be chosen while a crop is still growing. It takes effect at the next sowing rather than tearing out what is already in the ground.

Produce waits in the module until Harvest Hydroponics collects it. Varieties keep apart rather than mixing, so a harvest left standing is still itself when the next crop ripens beside it.

#### Xenoflora

<!-- updated: xenoflora -->

Alien life can be made to grow in a ship's beds, and what it grows is not food. A Xenoflora strain is tuned against a single hazard, and a ripe crop yields sealed doses that make a survivor resistant to it. The crew is running Adaptive Cultivar at the scale of a whole module.

None of this is the crew's to reach for. **The GM decides whether the crew has a viable alien specimen at all**, and until they say so the Xenoflora Cultivation project cannot be started. Nothing is owed: a specimen is a reward for a salvage run, a derelict, or a xenobiological find, and a GM who never puts one in play has a campaign where xenoflora simply does not exist.

Once the GM has granted a specimen, the crew still has to finish the project. It is run once; after that the beds will take a strain whenever the crew wants one.

| PROJECT: XENOFLORA CULTIVATION |
| --- |
| Progress Required: 6 |
| Skill: Wits + Botany; Routine Threshold 7 |
| Contributor Limit: 2; Energy per Contribution: 2 |
| Facility: Expanded Hydroponics or better, and a Biological Lab |
| Cost: Committed at the start — 1 extracted Research Trait, 2 Scrapped Biomass, 3 Chemical Mixture |
| Output: Hydroponics will accept a Xenoflora sowing |

A strain is tuned when it is sown, and that choice is fixed for the crop. Deciding what the crew will need three days from now is the whole of the gamble. A strain can be tuned against either a condition or a damage type:

- **Conditions:** Burning, Contaminated, Freezing, Overheated, Poisoned, Suffocating.
- **Damage:** Fire, Electric, Chemical. Ballistic and Physical are not on the list — no plant makes a survivor shrug off a bullet.

A dose of a condition strain makes its user Resistant to that condition for the expedition. A dose of a damage strain gives Resistance to that damage type, halving each hit of it for the expedition. Halving damage all expedition is worth a good deal more than shrugging off one condition, so a damage strain yields half as many doses, rounded down.

| Hydroponics | Condition doses | Damage doses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expanded | 3 | 1 |
| Self-Sustaining | 5 | 2 |

Xenoflora will not take root below Expanded Hydroponics. A ripe crop is not harvested — the doses are drawn and sealed as it ripens and go straight into the hold, where they keep until used. The beds then stand empty, because the ship's own routine will not put an alien strain into the ground unasked. Someone has to take Maintain Crops and sow it deliberately.

A strain resists automation rather than benefiting from it. **It needs Maintain Crops every single day, at every module level, including Self-Sustaining.** Miss a day and it does not stall like an ordinary crop: it slips containment. The crop is lost, the beds revert to Food, and the GM has something loose aboard the ship to run as they see fit.

### Projects

Projects are tasks too large to finish in one segment. Building a room, upgrading a module, conducting research, and restoring a ruined reactor are all Projects.

Every Project lists: required Progress, relevant skill, Routine Threshold, facilities, materials, and any deadline or consequence.

- A qualified Routine work segment adds 1 Progress automatically.
- A successful Skilled or Crisis work segment adds 1 Progress.
- Materials may be paid at the start, per Progress, or on completion, as specified by the Project.
- Failed work adds no Progress and may consume committed resources.
- Progress remains until completed unless damage or an event removes it.

| PROJECT EXAMPLE: EXPAND THE SHIP |
| --- |
| Progress Required: 8 |
| Skill: Engineering; Routine Threshold 3 |
| Facility: Functioning Workshop / Fabrication Bay |
| Cost: Paid once for each Progress according to the ship’s current Hull tier |
| A character with Mechanical Technician 5 completes one Progress automatically in a safe segment. A less-skilled character may roll Mechanical Technician to attempt the same work. |

### Events and Interruptions

The GM may introduce an event at the start of a segment, after tasks are committed, during resolution, or after tasks finish. The timing should follow the fiction and should be stated clearly.

- An event may convert a Routine task into a Skilled or Crisis task.
- An interrupted character may abandon their task and respond to the event, but committed Energy and materials are not automatically refunded.
- Characters whose tasks have not yet been resolved may reassign them when the event makes the original task impossible.
- Warnings, sensor data, and visible countdowns should be provided whenever the crew could reasonably detect them.

### Energy

Energy measures how much meaningful work a character can perform before exhaustion. Energy is spent on shipboard tasks and other strenuous actions. Combat uses its own action economy and does not normally spend Energy unless a rule says otherwise.

- Base Maximum Energy is 3.
- Every 2 Constitution above 1 increases Maximum Energy by 1.
- Energy cannot normally fall below 0.
- A character at 0 Energy is Exhausted and cannot begin another Energy-costing task unless they Push or an ability permits it.
- Temporary reductions to Maximum Energy also reduce current Energy if current Energy is above the new maximum.

#### Rest and Energy Recovery

<!-- updated: rest -->

Rest costs 0 Energy but consumes one segment. It can be taken anywhere safe; Crew Quarters aren't required. To benefit, the character must not be under immediate threat and must have consumed Food and Water within the previous day. Otherwise the Rest only counts as sleep.

Every Rest:

- Restores 1 HP.
- Restores all Energy.
- Lowers Pressure by 1, but not below the Stress floor.

### Daily Survival Requirements

At the End of Day, check each character’s Food, Water, Oxygen, and Sleep. Eating and drinking do not consume tasks and may occur during any segment.

| Requirement | Daily Need | First Miss | Second Consecutive Miss | Third Consecutive Miss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Food | 1 Food Unit | -1 Maximum Energy next day | -2 Maximum Energy next day; Rest restores no Energy | -2 Maximum Energy; Rest restores no Energy; gain 1 Stress |
| Water | 1 Water Unit | -2 Maximum Energy next day | Death without immediate intervention | Death |
| Oxygen | Continuous supply | Approximately 3 minutes before death; use scene timing | Death | Death |
| Sleep | One Rest or sleep segment | -1 Maximum Energy next day | -3 Maximum Energy next day | Collapse and spend the next 2 segments Resting |

#### Rationing

A character may consume half a Food Unit instead of one full unit. At the beginning of the next day, make a flat Constitution roll.

- Success: no additional penalty.
- Failure: Maximum Energy is reduced by 1 for that day.
- Two consecutive days of rationing, whether the rolls succeed or fail, cause 1 Stress.

### Pressure

Pressure represents immediate fear, adrenaline, tunnel vision, and survival instinct. It rises quickly during danger and fades when the immediate crisis passes.

Pressure has five states: Calm, Shaken, Pressured, Overtaxed, and Panicked. Characters begin Calm unless Stress or another rule establishes a higher starting state.

| State | Pressure Dice | Failure Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calm | +0d6 | No additional consequence. |
| Shaken | +1d6 | Become Pressured. |
| Pressured | +2d6 | Lose 1 current Energy after the roll, and gain 1 Stress. |
| Overtaxed | +3d6 | Lose all current Energy and gain 1 Pressure and 1 Stress after the roll |
| Panicked | +4d6 | Lose all current Energy, suffer 2 HP damage, gain 1 Stress, and trigger a Mind Break. |

Pressure Dice are added to skill rolls. They represent heightened focus and desperate effort. Pressure therefore makes a character more capable and more fragile at the same time.

<!-- updated: overtaxed-routine -->

Routine tasks are not rolled merely because a character has Pressure. However, Overtaxed or Panicked characters cannot perform Routine tasks automatically: their work becomes Skilled because they are no longer operating under controlled conditions.

#### Gaining Pressure

Gain one level of Pressure when a rule calls for it. Common triggers include:

<!-- updated: damage-pressure -->

- Pushing a task or roll.
- Losing 2 or more HP from one source (at most once per Round).
- Being surprised by a credible threat.
- Witnessing a grotesque or impossible event.
- Entering decompression, suffocation, uncontrolled fire, or imminent structural collapse.
- Seeing an ally fall to 0 HP or die.
- A personal Affliction being triggered.

If a character would gain Pressure while already Panicked, they immediately trigger another Mind Break and gain 1 Stress instead.

#### Reducing Pressure

<!-- updated: rest, stress-floor -->

- When an immediate danger clearly ends, reduce Pressure by 1.
- A successful Reduce Pressure task lowers another character’s Pressure by 1.
- Each Rest lowers Pressure by 1.
- At the beginning of a new day, Pressure resets to the Stress floor: Calm, or higher at Stress 5+.
- Special Brews, sedatives, abilities, cybernetics, and biological augmentations may modify Pressure as described in their own rules.

No reduction takes Pressure below the Stress floor, Rest included: at Stress 5+ Pressure can’t drop below Shaken, and at Stress 7+ not below Pressured.

### Pushing

A character may Push when ordinary effort is not enough. Pushing is a deliberate decision, not an automatic response to failure.

A Push may do one of the following:

- Reroll every failed die from one skill roll. Dice that already rolled 6 are kept.
- Continue acting when at 0 Energy, if the pushed task can still be attempted.

Each Push costs +1 Energy and immediately increases Pressure by 1. The Pressure increase occurs before a rerolled check is resolved, so the new Pressure Dice and failure consequence apply to the reroll.

<!-- updated: exhausted-push -->

A character at 0 Energy may still Push. Instead of the Energy, the Push costs an Affliction (roll on the Afflictions table), plus the usual +1 Pressure.

- A roll may be pushed only once.
- A character may gain only one additional task from Pushing in a segment.
- Routine tasks do not require a reroll, but a character may Push to perform an extra Routine task.
- A Panicked character may Push, but gaining Pressure while Panicked triggers the normal overflow consequence.

### Stress

Stress represents lasting psychological strain. It is tracked from 0 to 10 and does not disappear when danger ends or after ordinary sleep.

Pressure asks, “Can I survive this moment?” Stress asks, “How much of this can I carry forward?”

<!-- updated: stress-floor, strained, breaking -->

| Stress | Condition | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-2 | Stable | No additional effect. |
| 3-4 | Strained | The first time you gain Pressure each day, gain one additional level. This carries into every higher Stress state. |
| 5-6 | Frayed | Begin each expedition Shaken. Pressure cannot drop below Shaken. |
| 7-8 | Unstable | Begin each expedition Pressured. Pressure cannot drop below Pressured. The first Mind Break each expedition also causes an Affliction. |
| 9 | Breaking | Every Mind Break causes an Affliction. |
| 10 | Collapsed | Immediately gain an Affliction. Stress cannot increase further; every further Stress gain causes a Mind Break instead. |

Stress sets a Pressure floor at all times, not only at the start of the day: at Stress 5+ Pressure can't drop below Shaken, and at Stress 7+ it can't drop below Pressured.

#### Gaining Stress

Stress is gained from events that leave a lasting mark, not from every failed roll.

<!-- updated: deprivation-stress -->

| Event | Stress Gained |
| --- | --- |
| Triggering a Mind Break while Panicked | 1 |
| Witnessing a crewmate die | 1 |
| Suffering a permanent injury or losing a limb or sense | 1 |
| Failing Surgery on a Major or Deadly injury in a catastrophic way | 1 |
| The third day without sufficient Food or sleep, and every third day after | 1 |
| Completing an expedition in which the character reached Panicked | 1, maximum once per expedition |
| Respawning, being reconstructed, or returning from confirmed death | 2 |
| Causing a mass casualty event or confronting a uniquely catastrophic revelation | 2-4, GM discretion |

The same event should not award Stress repeatedly unless it represents a new trauma. Willpower may prevent stress as described below.

#### Recovering Stress

Stress is reduced through deliberate recovery, not ordinary Rest. Stress Recovery is a Project requiring safety, time, and support.

| STRESS RECOVERY PROJECT |
| --- |
| Progress Required: 3 |
| Task Energy: 2 per Progress |
| Skill: Entertainer 3 required for Routine work |
| Requirements: Functioning Crew Quarters or Medical Bay, full Food and Water for the day for the attempted stress target, and no active immediate threat |
| Completion: Reduce the patient’s Stress by 1d4 + 2. |
| Psychiatric Medication may provide 1 automatic Progress once per day. A Special Brew may provide +1d6 to a recovery roll, but cannot complete the Project by itself. |

Stress Recovery Progress is lost if the patient gains Stress before the Project is completed. A character may be the patient in only one Stress Recovery task per day.

### Willpower

Willpower represents a character’s capacity to refuse collapse when the body or mind should give way. Base Maximum Willpower is 1. Every 3 Constitution above 1 increases Maximum Willpower by 1.

Spend 1 Willpower to do one of the following:

<!-- updated: willpower -->

- Prevent 1 Stress immediately after it would be gained.
- Contest a Mind Break with a flat Constitution roll. On success, delay the Mind Break until the end of the current round or task, then reduce Pressure to Overtaxed.
- Reroll a failed death check while at 0 HP.
- Remain conscious until the end of the current round after an effect would make you collapse.
- Resist a Behavioral condition.

<!-- updated: act -->

Willpower cannot reduce Pressure directly. All spent Willpower is restored when the character completes a Stress Recovery Project or completes an Act. An Act is a chapter of the campaign built around one major goal or threat; the GM declares it complete when the crew resolves that goal, usually every 3–5 sessions.

### Mind Breaks

A Mind Break is a temporary loss of control caused by extreme Pressure. Unless a rule states otherwise, roll 1d6 when a Mind Break triggers. The effect lasts until the character falls unconscious, the immediate danger ends, or another character successfully stabilizes them with a Crisis First Aid or Entertainer task. If a Mind Break contests a forced action from an affliction, the Mind Break takes precedent.

| d6 | Mind Break | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Freeze Response | The character cannot move, speak, or take actions and is helpless. |
| 2 | Total Shutdown | The character collapses unconscious. They awaken only after the immediate danger ends or successful medical intervention. |
| 3 | Flight Instinct | The character must flee by the safest apparent route, abandoning plans and equipment if necessary. |
| 4 | Dissociation | All actions suffer -6d6. The character may not recognize allies, threats, or surroundings correctly. |
| 5 | Compulsive Shedding | The character attempts to remove worn equipment, armor, clothing, or implants that feel foreign, regardless of danger. |
| 6 | Violent Outburst | The character attacks the nearest perceived threat recklessly. After that target is down or unreachable, all rolls suffer -6d6 until the break ends. |

## Afflictions

Afflictions are lasting psychological conditions created by trauma, prolonged Stress, repeated Mind Breaks, deprivation, and exposure to situations the mind cannot cleanly process.

Afflictions are stylized game mechanics rather than clinical diagnoses.

An Affliction may:

- Trigger Pressure.
- Apply conditions.
- Create compulsive behavior.
- Alter how a character responds to particular damage types.
- Disable a condition Resistance or Immunity.
- Restrict certain weapons, equipment, or actions.
- Distort information provided by the GM.
- Interact with cybernetics, biological augmentations, mechs, and Background abilities.

Removing a condition caused by an Affliction does not cure the Affliction itself.

### Gaining an Affliction

When a character gains an Affliction:

- Roll 1d6 to determine the Affliction category.
- Roll 1d10 to determine the Affliction within that category.
- Record the Affliction and its triggers.
- If the result requires a chosen damage type, condition, character, or behavior, make that choice immediately.

If a character rolls an Affliction they already possess, reroll the d10.

The GM may select an Affliction instead of rolling when a specific traumatic event clearly supports one result.

### Affliction Conditions

Conditions caused by an Affliction follow the normal condition rules.

Unless an Affliction says otherwise:

- Condition Resistance and Immunity still function.
- Reapplying the same condition refreshes its duration.
- An Affliction-triggered condition does not stack with itself.
- Pressure states are not conditions.

Some Afflictions explicitly disable a Resistance or Immunity. These effects override benefits from Backgrounds, cybernetics, biological augmentations, mechs, and all other possible sources.

## Afflictions and Damage Types

When an Affliction prevents the use of a damage type:

- The character cannot intentionally make attacks or activate abilities that primarily deal that damage type.
- The character may still carry, transfer, repair, or deconstruct related equipment.
- Accidental environmental damage does not violate the restriction.
- The character may use the equipment for a non-damaging purpose if reasonable.
- An ally cannot simply order the character to ignore the Affliction.

When an Affliction reduces an Immunity to Resistance:

- An Immune target becomes Resistant.
- If no specific Resistance rule is listed, applying the condition requires one additional success.
- The underlying Immunity returns when the Affliction is cured.

When an Affliction causes a condition, removing that condition does not prevent the Affliction from triggering again later.

## Treating Afflictions

Afflictions are not removed by ordinary Rest, First Aid, condition removal, or Pressure reduction.

| AFFLICTION TREATMENT PROJECT |
| --- |
| Progress Required: 5 consecutive daily Progress |
| Skill: Surgery 4 or Entertainer 4; this task is always Skilled |
| Requirements: Medical Bay or equivalent therapeutic facility, Psychiatric Medication or Special Brew each day, and a willing patient |
| Completion: Remove one random curable Affliction |
| Interruption: Missing a day or gaining a new Affliction resets Progress to 0 |
| Taking more than one dose of Psychiatric Medication or Special Brew in a day deals 1 HP damage. |

Injuries represent physical harm that persists beyond lost HP. They describe lasting damage such as fractures, burns, damaged organs, torn muscles, and lost limbs.

<!-- updated: injury-threshold -->

Roll for an Injury whenever a single hit takes 3 or more HP after Cover and Armor, whenever a character drops to 0 HP, or when a rule explicitly forces an Injury, unless the source states otherwise.

<!-- updated: injuries, injury-severity -->

Roll 2d6:

- The first die sets the Injury's Severity.
- The second die sets its Location: 1 Head, 2 Senses, 3 Arms, 4 Legs, 5 Torso, 6 Internal.

| First Die | Severity |
| --- | --- |
| 1–3 | Minor Injury |
| 4–5 | Major Injury |
| 6 | Deadly Injury |

Together the two dice pick one of the 36 Injuries on the Injury table in the Adrift Compendium.

A character carries at most 2 Minor Injuries and 1 Major Injury at a time. Going over that limit, or rolling an Injury the character already has, makes the new Injury one step worse in the same Location: a Minor result becomes Major, and a Major result becomes Deadly.

#### Injury Severity

<!-- updated: injuries -->

Each Injury lists the rolls it affects. Severity sets the penalty:

- Minor Injuries impose −1d6 on the rolls they list.
- Major Injuries impose −2d6 on the rolls they list and apply the condition they list until Surgery. The character starts Bleeding.
- Deadly Injuries impose −2d6 on the rolls they list and are catastrophic: either the character needs Surgery by the end of the next day segment or dies, or the Injury is a permanent loss that only a cybernetic or graft replaces. The character starts Bleeding.

The penalty lasts until the Injury heals, even once it is Treated.

Injuries do not automatically reduce Maximum HP, Maximum Energy, or increase the Energy cost of unrelated Tasks.

#### Injury States

<!-- updated: injury-severity, treated-injuries -->

Injuries are either Untreated or Treated.

Untreated: The Injury deals 1 HP at the End of Day.

Treated: The Injury no longer deals HP at the End of Day, but keeps its dice penalty until it heals. First Aid's first 6 treats an Untreated Injury of any Severity. Major and Deadly Injuries still need Surgery, which turns them into Treated Minor Injuries.

<!-- updated: injuries -->

### Duplicate Injuries

Rolling an Injury the character already has makes it one step worse in the same Location, as does going over the limit of 2 Minor and 1 Major Injury.

#### Natural Recovery

<!-- updated: natural-recovery -->

Each time a character Rests, if they have at least one Treated Minor Injury, they make one recovery roll. No Crew Quarters are needed.

Roll a number of d6 equal to your Constitution + the number of days since the oldest Treated Minor Injury was received. On any 6, remove that Injury. Major and Deadly Injuries made Minor by Surgery count; permanent losses never heal this way.

#### Bleeding and First Aid

<!-- updated: bleeding, first-aid -->

When a character receives a Major or Deadly Injury, they start Bleeding.

Bleeding ends with a successful First Aid task or by spending an appropriate Medkit use.

First Aid is a free action, usable any time outside the segment structure, with no Energy cost, but it uses up one medkit from the healer's inventory or ship storage. The kit heals its own HP (Improvised Medkit 1, Medkit 3, Glitterworld Medkit 5) and adds its dice (Medkit +1d6, Glitterworld Medkit +3d6). It is always a Skilled roll:

- If treating an Untreated Injury of any Severity, the first 6 treats it. A Major or Deadly Injury still needs Surgery to become Minor.
- Every other 6 heals 1 HP or removes one condition.
- First Aid works with no Injury at all, purely to heal.
- Giving First Aid to another player's character needs that player's agreement.
- In combat, First Aid is a Slow Action.

First Aid cannot repair permanent structural damage, restore lost organs, or replace Surgery.

#### Surgery

<!-- updated: surgery -->

Surgery is always a Crisis task. It requires a functioning Medical Bay, Surgical Tools, appropriate supplies, and another character to operate. A Mobile Surgical Cradle removes the need for a Medical Bay, but doesn't make Surgery easier.

- When Surgery begins, the patient drops to half their Maximum HP if above it, regardless of the outcome.
- The patient loses 2 current Energy, to a minimum of 0.
- If already at 1 HP, the patient collapses and must spend the next 2 segments resting after the procedure.
- Success converts one Major or Deadly injury into a treated Minor injury unless the injury says otherwise.
- Failure gives the patient one additional Minor injury and 1 Stress. The original injury remains.
- Additional successes may restore 1 HP, remove a separate Minor injury, or reduce a lasting penalty, according to the Surgery stunt list.

#### Death

At 0 HP, a character is down but not immediately dead. At the end of each combat round or equivalent scene interval, make a flat Constitution roll. On failure, the character dies. One Willpower may be spent to reroll that failed check.

A character at 0 HP cannot perform normal tasks and is vulnerable to further harm. Any additional HP damage may cause immediate death when the fiction supports it.

<!-- updated: stabilize -->

First Aid on a character at 0 HP needs a 6 to stabilize them, which uses that 6. A stabilized character stops making death checks. Any healing above 0 HP, the medkit's own healing included, wakes them; new damage ends the stabilization.

### End of Day Procedure

1. Check Food and Water consumption.

2. Check whether each character completed a Rest or sleep segment.

3. Apply starvation, dehydration, sleep, untreated injury, and other End of Day effects.

4. Resolve natural Minor Injury recovery.

5. Advance effects measured in days, including rationing streaks, treatment Projects, contamination, and deadlines.

6. Restore Energy for characters who completed at least one valid Rest, subject to penalties.

7. Reset Pressure to the minimum allowed by the character’s Stress.

8. Begin the next Morning.

## Chapter 3: Making Do and Desperation

Crafting and Items

Survival in Adrift is not just about what you carry into a fight - it’s about what you can build when everything breaks.

Crafting allows the crew to turn scrap, salvaged components, and raw supplies into tools, consumables, and ship systems. Nothing is permanent. Every item exists to solve a problem right now, often at the cost of creating another problem later.

### Crafting

Crafting represents time, effort, and risk spent creating an item from available materials.

To craft an item, a character must:

- Have access to the required materials
- Meet any tool or module requirements listed in the recipe
- Spend the required Energy
- Succeed on the appropriate skill roll

Each recipe lists:

- Skill: The Skill used if a roll is required.
- Routine Threshold: The Skill Level required to craft the item without rolling under normal conditions.
- Energy: The Energy spent by the character performing the work.
- Time: The number of segments required.
- Tools: The Toolkit or specialized equipment required.
- Facility: Any room or ship module required.
- Materials: The resources consumed.
- Output: What the recipe creates.
- Failure: Any consequence unique to the recipe.

To begin crafting, a character must:

- Have access to the listed materials.
- Have access to the required tools.
- Have access to the required facility, if one is listed.
- Spend the listed Energy.
- Spend the listed time.

Most basic crafting requires an Improvised Toolkit or better.

Advanced recipes may require a functioning Workshop, Medical Bay, Laboratory, or another specialized facility. The full rules for ship facilities are described in Chapter 4.

Crafting can also be done on the fly during an expedition, if necessary. Rules for this will be up to the GM’s discretion, but will require a much higher difficulty.

### Routine Crafting

Crafting is Routine when the character meets the recipe’s Routine Threshold and possesses the required materials, tools, facility, time, and safe working conditions.

Routine crafting automatically succeeds.

No roll is made.

Being 3 levels above the routine crafting threshold for an item reduces the energy cost to produce it by 1.

<!-- updated: fabricate -->

Fabricating makes two items in the same segment. The task's Energy cost covers both of them, not one each.

Being 6 levels above the Routine crafting threshold for every item lets you make a third one in the same segment, covered by the same Energy cost.

### Skilled Crafting

If the character does not meet the Routine Threshold, they may still attempt the recipe as a Skilled Task.

Crafting also becomes Skilled when the character lacks ideal tools, uses substitute materials, works in a damaged facility, rushes the recipe, alters the design, or encounters another source of meaningful uncertainty.

Resolve the Task using the recipe’s listed Skill.

One success creates the item. Each level below the required skill level the crafter is under subtracts 1 d6 from the attempted roll.

### Failed Crafting

When a Skilled crafting Task fails:

- The item is not created.
- The character still spends the listed Energy and time.
- Any required Toolkit charges are consumed.
- Lose 50% of the recipe’s materials, rounded up.
- Return the remaining materials to storage.

A failed roll represents ruined fittings, damaged components, contaminated supplies, wasted sealant, or work that must be started again. Some unstable, experimental, or alien recipes may list harsher failure consequences.

### Deconstructing Items

Items may be dismantled to recover usable materials.

Deconstruction uses the Task rules from Chapter 2 and normally uses the Scavenging Skill.

To deconstruct an item, a character must:

- Have access to an Improvised Toolkit or better.
- Spend one segment.
- Spend the listed Energy, if any.
- Have enough space to safely dismantle the item.

Each item has a deconstruction Routine Threshold based on its complexity.

| Item Type | Routine Threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Basic or Tier I equipment | Scavenging 2 |
| Tier II equipment | Scavenging 4 |
| Tier III equipment | Scavenging 6 |
| Alien or experimental equipment | Scavenging 7 or higher, after study |

#### Routine Deconstruction

Routine deconstruction automatically recovers approximately 50% of the item’s recoverable material cost, rounded down.

#### Skilled Deconstruction

If the character does not meet the Routine Threshold, they may attempt the work as a Skilled Task.

On a success, recover 50% of the item’s recoverable materials, rounded down.

For each additional success, recover another 25% of the original material cost, up to a maximum of 100%.

#### Deconstruction Limits

The following are not normally recovered:

- Fuel already consumed.
- Food or water.
- Expended chemicals.
- Used medicine.
- Destroyed ammunition.
- Contaminated biological material.
- Unique components that were destroyed during use.
- Toolkit charges.

### Inventory and Carrying Capacity

During expeditions, every kilogram matters.

Each character has a limited number of inventory slots equal to:

Strength + one-third of Scavenging Level, rounded down

For example, a character with Strength 3 and Scavenging 4 has:

3 + 1 = 4 Inventory Slots

#### Item Sizes

Items may have one of the following sizes:

- Compact: Small enough to share a slot when specifically allowed.
- Standard: Occupies 1 slot.
- Bulky: Occupies 2 slots.
- Heavy: Occupies 3 or more slots and may require assistance to move.

A character cannot knowingly begin an expedition while carrying more items than their available slots allow.

Excess items must be:

- Stored aboard the ship.
- Given to another character.
- Loaded into specialized carrying equipment.
- Dropped.
- Left behind.

Ship storage and Cargo modules are described in Chapter 4.

### Transferring and Dropping Items

While aboard the ship or in another safe environment, characters may freely move ordinary items between storage and their personal inventory.

During an expedition:

- Giving an item to an adjacent character is normally a Fast Action.
- Picking up an accessible item is normally a Fast Action.
- Removing a packed or secured item may require a Slow Action.
- Dropping an item is free unless the circumstances make doing so difficult.

Items left behind remain where they were dropped unless another creature or environmental effect moves them.

### Crafting During Expeditions

Crafting and deconstruction are safest aboard the ship.

Attempting either during an expedition is possible, but dangerous.

Unless the GM determines the area is secure and properly equipped, expedition crafting is treated as a Crisis Task.

Expedition crafting:

- Always requires a roll.
- Suffers a −3d6 penalty.
- Produces noticeable noise.
- May produce light, heat, sparks, chemical odors, or electronic interference.
- Uses the normal crafting failure rules.
- Creates an immediate complication on failure when danger is nearby.

Possible complications include:

- Attracting an enemy.
- Damaging the item being repaired.
- Rupturing a spacesuit or seal.
- Starting a fire.
- Contaminating materials.
- Increasing Pressure.
- Losing access to the workspace.
- Consuming an additional Toolkit charge.

A portable Workshop or specialized field equipment may reduce or remove some of these penalties, but does not make work Routine during immediate danger.

### Emergency Repairs

Some damaged items can be temporarily restored during an expedition.

An emergency repair does not fully repair the item.

Instead, it allows the item to function until:

- The expedition ends.
- The item is used a listed number of times.
- The item suffers further damage.
- The GM determines the temporary repair fails.

Emergency repairs are normally Crisis Tasks.

They require:

- An appropriate Toolkit.
- Plausible replacement materials.
- One Slow Action or more, depending on the damage.
- A successful Mechanical Technician, Engineering, or other appropriate Skill roll.

A failed emergency repair may worsen the damage.

Permanent repairs must be performed using the item’s normal repair recipe in an appropriate facility.

### Consumables, Charges, and Uses

Many items are limited by charges or uses.

#### Charges

A charge represents a limited amount of usable material, power, medicine, ammunition, or operating life.

Each use consumes one charge unless the item states otherwise.

When an item reaches zero charges, it becomes:

- Empty.
- Depleted.
- Unpowered.
- Broken.
- Otherwise unusable.

The exact state depends on the item.

Some items may be recharged or refilled through crafting.

Others are destroyed when their last charge is used.

#### Consumables

Consumables are destroyed or permanently expended when used unless stated otherwise.

Examples include:

- Medicine.
- Food.
- Fuel.
- Ammunition.
- Chemical treatments.
- Single-use repair compounds.
- Temporary biological compounds.

#### Reusable Equipment

Reusable equipment remains after use, but may require:

- Charges.
- Ammunition.
- Fuel.
- Maintenance.
- Repair.
- Cleaning.
- Recalibration.

An item does not degrade merely because it was used unless its rules state otherwise.

Damage, failed Tasks, environmental hazards, or specific enemy effects may reduce an item’s functionality.

### Broken and Depleted Items

A depleted item has no remaining charges but may otherwise be intact.

A broken item has suffered physical or functional damage.

These states are different.

A depleted Toolkit may need replacement supplies.

A broken Toolkit may need replacement parts.

An item may be both broken and depleted.

Repairing an item restores its physical function.

Recharging or refilling it restores its uses.

Each may require a separate recipe.

### Advanced Equipment

Some equipment follows additional rules beyond ordinary crafting.

#### Cybernetics

Cybernetics are invasive mechanical replacements installed through Surgery. Their recipes and long-term consequences are described later.

#### Biological Augmentations

Biological augmentations are living replacements and modifications created from alien material. They interact with Pressure, Stress, and the character’s physiology. Their full rules are described later.

## Chapter 4: Keeping Her Flying

## Ship Mechanics

Your ship is not a backdrop. It is shelter, transportation, workshop, hospital, farm, weapons platform, and the last barrier between the crew and empty space.

Every room aboard consumes power. Every system requires maintenance. Every expansion demands time and resources that could have been spent elsewhere. A larger ship gives the crew more options, but it also creates more things that can fail.

This chapter explains:

- Rooms and modules.
- Power generation and consumption.
- Facility Grades.
- Module condition.
- Ship construction Projects.
- Hull HP and Armor Plating.
- Ship damage and repair.
- Storage and cargo.
- Movement and scanning.
- Internal defenses and ship weapons.

The general rules for Tasks, Routine Thresholds, Projects, Energy, assistance, and Crisis work are described in Chapter 2.

Crafting portable items, Toolkit charges, deconstruction, and expedition inventory are described in Chapter 3.

### Rooms and Modules

The ship is divided into rooms.

Each room may support one primary module unless another rule states otherwise.

A room represents the structure surrounding the module: pressure seals, wiring, coolant lines, access corridors, control systems, and the physical space required for the module to function.

Examples of modules include:

- Fusion Reactor.
- Life Support.
- Propulsion and Engine Section.
- Navigation and Sensor Suite.
- Crew Quarters.
- Workshop or Fabrication Bay.
- Medical Bay.
- Hydroponics.
- Cargo Storage.
- Armory.
- Defensive System.
- Ship Weapon.
- Biological Laboratory.
- Other specialized facilities.

An empty room may be used for ordinary storage or temporary activity, but it provides no specialized module benefit.

#### One Room, One Module

A room normally supports one module.

Small equipment, personal storage, furniture, access panels, and ordinary controls do not count as additional modules.

A module occupying several rooms states this in its construction requirements.

<!-- updated: room-fittings -->

Alongside its module, a room has one spare slot for a fitting: Makeshift Solar Panels, Traps, or a Defensive Gun. A room holds one of the three and no more, so panels and defenses compete for the same space. Fitting one is the Install Panels or Defenses Task rather than a Project: one Task, the materials the upgrade sheet lists, and a Workshop to work from.

#### Module Requirements

Each module entry should list:

- Grade: The module’s level of sophistication.
- Power Draw: The amount of ship power required.
- Crew Requirement: Whether someone must actively operate it.
- Facility Functions: What Tasks or recipes it supports.
- Routine Benefits: Any Tasks it makes easier or possible.
- Condition Effects: What happens when it becomes Damaged or Critical.
- Construction Project: Progress, tools, materials, and Skill required.
- Repair Profile: Materials and Progress needed to restore it.

#### Installing a Module

Constructing a room does not automatically install a module.

Once the room is complete, the crew must complete the module’s Construction Project.

An installed module does not function until:

- Its Construction Project is complete.
- The room is pressurized, if required.
- The module is connected to ship power.
- Any required calibration or activation Task has been completed.

Removing an intact module requires a Deconstruction or Relocation Project.

The recovered materials depend on the module’s condition and the deconstruction rules in Chapter 3.

### Power

Most ship modules require power.

Power is measured in Power Units.

Each Reactor lists how many Power Units it produces. Each module lists how many Power Units it consumes while active.

A module with no listed Power Draw does not require meaningful ship power or includes its own minor supply.

#### Power Draw

Power Draw uses the following general scale:

| Power Draw | Typical Use |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | Passive rooms, storage, simple defenses |
| 1 | Crew Quarters, Kitchen, basic Workshop, basic sensors |
| 2 | Medical Bay, Hydroponics, advanced Workshop, active defenses |
| 3 | Engine Section, major weapons, advanced laboratories |
| 4+ | Experimental systems, extreme weapons, unique technology |

Individual modules may differ.

#### Running the Grid

<!-- updated: daily-power, grid-cell -->

Keeping the ship powered spends one day of a Power Cell's charge, taken at End of Day. A cell holds as many days of ship power as it moves the ship in tiles:

| Power Cell | Days of ship power |
| --- | --- |
| Flickering Power Cell | 1 |
| Power Cell | 2 |
| Advanced Power Cell | 4 |

When the loaded cell runs out, the next one takes over. The crew decides which kind the grid runs on, and may change their minds at any time. Left to itself the grid takes the weakest cell in the hold, so stronger ones stay available for travel and mechs.

Either choice costs something. Burning Flickering cells keeps the good ones free for the engines, but it means feeding the grid again every single night and it is the first thing to fail when the hold runs thin. Putting an Advanced Power Cell in is four quiet days and one less thing to remember, paid for with the cell that would have carried the ship four tiles.

If the crew asks for a kind the hold has run out of, the grid loads the weakest cell it has rather than letting the ship go dark. With no charge and no cell aboard at all, the ship runs dark.

#### Power Capacity

At the beginning of each day, compare:

Total Power Generated

against

Total Power Draw of Active Modules

If generation meets or exceeds demand, all active modules function normally.

If demand exceeds generation, the crew must deactivate modules until the ship is within capacity.

Deactivating or activating an accessible module is normally free during planning aboard a stable ship.

It may become a Task during:

- Combat.
- A power surge.
- Reactor damage.
- Remote-control failure.
- Decompression.
- Another active Crisis.

#### Unpowered Modules

An unpowered module is Inactive.

Inactive is not a damage condition.

An Inactive module:

- Provides no benefits.
- Cannot support Tasks or recipes.
- Does not consume power.
- Retains its current physical condition.
- Returns to normal when power is restored.

Some modules require a startup Task after being unpowered.

Life Support, Reactors, Engines, and experimental systems may list special shutdown consequences.

#### Essential Systems

The crew may designate systems as essential.

Common essential systems include:

- Life Support.
- Atmospheric regulation.
- Reactor controls.
- Emergency lighting.
- Navigation during travel.
- Medical systems during Surgery.

Essential designation does not create additional power.

It determines which modules remain active when power must be cut.

#### Power Overload

The crew may deliberately operate above normal Power Capacity for one segment.

This is called Overloading the Grid.

For that segment:

- The ship may supply up to 2 additional Power Units.
- The Reactor becomes Critical for operational purposes.
- All Tasks involving powered systems become Crisis Tasks.
- At the end of the segment, make an Engineering Task using the Reactor’s listed threshold.

On a success, the overload ends without further damage.

On a failure:

- The Reactor becomes Damaged, or worsens one condition if already Damaged.
- One active module becomes Offline until repaired.
- Everyone in or near the affected systems gains 1 Pressure.
- The GM may introduce fire, radiation, electrical injury, or another appropriate consequence.

A Reactor already at Critical condition cannot safely Overload the Grid.

Attempting it risks immediate catastrophic failure.

### Facility Grades

Facilities are modules designed to support specialized work.

Examples include:

- Workshops.
- Medical Bays.
- Biological Laboratories.
- Kitchens.
- Hydroponics.
- Sensor Suites.
- Neutronium Forges.
- Cybernetic Surgery Suites.

A character’s Skill determines whether work is reliable.

The Facility determines whether the work is physically possible.

#### Facility Grade

Facilities are divided into five broad Grades.

| Facility Grade | Capability |
| --- | --- |
| Makeshift | Emergency work and limited Tier I Tasks |
| Grade I | Reliable Tier I work |
| Grade II | Tier II work and advanced repairs |
| Grade III | Tier III work and highly controlled fabrication |
| Experimental | Alien, prototype, biological, or unique work |

Recipes and Tasks list the minimum Facility Grade required.

Meeting the Facility requirement does not automatically make a Task Routine. The lead character must still meet the Routine Threshold and all other requirements.

#### Grade I Facilities

Grade I facilities provide dependable professional equipment.

They generally support:

- Tier I fabrication.
- Basic repairs.
- Ordinary medicine.
- Standard food preparation.
- Basic analysis.
- Common ship maintenance.

#### Grade II Facilities

Grade II facilities provide improved automation, environmental control, and precision.

They generally support:

- Tier II fabrication.
- Major equipment repair.
- Advanced medicine.
- Reinforced Steel construction.
- Complex biological analysis.
- Reliable component production.

#### Grade III Facilities

Grade III facilities represent military, industrial, or cutting-edge equipment.

They generally support:

- Tier III fabrication.
- Neutronium construction.
- Quantum Components.
- Advanced reactor work.
- High-energy systems.
- Sophisticated cybernetics.
- Controlled experimental research.

#### Experimental Facilities

Experimental facilities support work outside normal human engineering or medical standards.

They may be required for:

- Alien machinery.
- Biological augmentation.
- Wormhole research.
- Living ship systems.
- Prototype weapons.
- Unstable materials.
- Other unique Projects.

An Experimental facility does not make unknown work Routine by itself.

Technology the crew does not understand may remain Skilled or Crisis regardless of Skill or Facility Grade.

#### Working Below the Required Grade

A facility one Grade below a Task’s requirement may be used if the Task can plausibly be improvised.

Doing so:

- Converts the Task into a Skilled Task.
- Applies −3d6.
- May consume an additional Toolkit charge.
- May impose a drawback on the finished work.

A facility two or more Grades below the requirement cannot support the Task unless the entry explicitly allows it.

### Module Condition

Ship modules use four common conditions:

- Operational.
- Damaged.
- Critical.
- Offline.

These conditions describe physical or functional damage.

A module that is merely unpowered is Inactive, not Offline.

#### Operational

The module functions normally.

It provides all listed benefits and supports Routine work when the other requirements are met.

#### Damaged

The module still functions, but unreliably.

Unless its entry states otherwise:

- Tasks using it suffer −2d6.
- Work that would normally be Routine becomes Skilled.
- Its output may be reduced.
- Its Power Draw remains unchanged.
- The GM may introduce minor faults related to the damage.

#### Critical

The module barely functions.

While Critical:

- It cannot support Routine work.
- All Tasks using it are Skilled or Crisis.
- Tasks using it suffer −3d6 unless the module lists another penalty.
- Failure may worsen it to Offline.
- Using it may create Pressure, injury, contamination, fire, or another immediate consequence.
- Its output is substantially reduced.

#### Offline

The module cannot function.

An Offline module:

- Provides no benefits.
- Cannot support Tasks.
- Cannot be operated.
- May block access to its room.
- May create an ongoing hazard.
- Requires repair before returning to service.

Offline does not always mean destroyed.

A destroyed module may require reconstruction rather than repair.

#### Worsening Condition

When a module worsens one condition:

Operational → Damaged → Critical → Offline

A module already Offline that suffers additional severe damage may be destroyed.

#### Restoring Condition

Repairs restore one condition at a time unless a rule states otherwise:

Offline → Critical → Damaged → Operational

Each step may require different materials, Project Progress, or replacement components.

Temporary emergency repairs may restore limited function without improving the recorded condition.

### Ship Projects

Large shipboard work uses the Project rules from Chapter 2.

Ship Projects include:

- Constructing rooms.
- Installing modules.
- Upgrading modules.
- Rebuilding destroyed systems.
- Reinforcing the hull.
- Expanding cargo.
- Relocating machinery.
- Performing major research.
- Integrating alien technology.

#### Ship Project Entry

Each Ship Project lists:

- Skill
- Routine Threshold
- Progress Required
- Contributor Limit
- Energy per Contribution
- Required Facility
- Required Toolkit
- Upfront Materials
- Materials per Progress
- Power Requirement
- Completion Result
- Failure or Interruption Effects

#### Project Contributions

Each contributor spends one Task during the segment.

A Routine contribution adds 1 Progress automatically.

A successful Skilled contribution adds 1 Progress.

Additional successes and Routine Benefits may add additional Progress as described in Chapters 2 and 3.

Unless stated otherwise:

- One character may add no more than 2 Progress per segment.
- Existing Progress is not lost from an ordinary failed contribution.
- Per-Progress materials are consumed only when Progress is added.
- Energy and Toolkit charges are spent even when a rolled contribution fails.

#### Contributor Limits

Most Ship Projects allow two contributors per segment.

Larger Projects may permit more.

Two characters contributing independently are not Assisting one another. Each resolves their own Project Task.

<!-- updated: assist, labor-projects -->

A character may instead Assist another contributor using the normal rules. They make no contribution of their own, but a successful Assist adds 1 Progress if the assistant meets the Project's Routine Threshold, and that takes one of the Project's contributor slots.

On a Project that builds, moves, or repairs something physical, an assistant with no useful skill may lend their back instead: Strength 3, adding 1 Progress only if the contributor they help is working the Project as Routine. See Lending a Back.

#### Occupied Workspaces

A partially completed module or major repair may occupy its room.

While occupied:

- The room cannot support another module.
- The unfinished system provides no benefits.
- Materials already installed cannot be freely used elsewhere.
- Abandoning the Project requires deconstruction.

#### Interrupting a Project

A Project may be paused without losing Progress.

However, exposed or unstable Projects may list consequences for remaining incomplete.

Examples include:

- An unfinished hull section cannot hold pressure.
- A partially connected Reactor draws no power but creates radiation risk.
- An incomplete biological chamber may become contaminated.
- An interrupted Life Support repair may reduce oxygen generation.

#### Abandoning a Project

When a Constrution Project is abandoned:

- Existing Progress is removed.
- Unspent stored materials are recovered.
- Materials already consumed through Progress are recovered using normal deconstruction rules.
- Unique components remain recoverable only if the Project allows it.
- The workspace becomes available after dismantling is completed.

### Expanding the Ship

The ship may be expanded by constructing additional rooms.

Each new room is a Ship Project requiring 8 Progress.

A completed room is empty until a module is installed.

#### Expand Ship

Skill: Engineering

Routine Threshold: Engineering 4

Progress Required: 8

Contributor Limit: 3

Energy per Contribution: 3

Facility: Operational Workshop

Toolkit: Any appropriate Toolkit, 1 charge per Progress

Power Requirement: None during construction

Output: One empty room

#### Materials per Progress

The material cost depends on the ship’s current Armor Plating tier.

##### Barely Standing or Patched Hull

Per Progress:

- 3 Scrap Metal.
- 1 Toolkit charge.

##### Reinforced Hull

Per Progress:

- 2 Reinforced Steel.
- 1 Toolkit charge.

##### Plated Hull

Per Progress:

- 1 Neutronium.
- 1 Toolkit charge.

Materials are consumed only when Progress is added.

If a contribution fails, Energy and Toolkit charges are spent, but structural materials are not consumed unless the failure consequence states otherwise.

#### Construction Requirements

The room must be built adjacent to an existing section of the ship.

The crew must determine:

- Where the room connects.
- Whether it requires an airlock.
- How it receives power.
- Whether it requires atmosphere.
- Whether its placement affects other rooms.

A room cannot be completed if its construction would make the ship physically impossible or sever required access.

#### Finishing the Room

When the Project reaches 8 Progress:

- The room becomes structurally complete.
- It is sealed and pressurized if Life Support can support it.
- It may receive power connections.
- It becomes available for storage or module construction.

The crew must complete a separate Project to install a module.

#### Exposed Construction

Until the room reaches 8 Progress, it is not fully sealed.

Damage to the construction area may:

- Destroy one or more Progress.
- Consume additional structural materials.
- Cause decompression.
- Damage an adjacent room.
- Force the Project to become Crisis work.

<!-- updated: acrobatics-rigging -->

Rig Safety Lines prevents that last consequence. A character who spends a Task stringing tethers and handholds through the open section keeps work there off Crisis for the rest of the day. The same Task makes a Weightless or unpressurized space workable, since ordinary movement in one otherwise demands Acrobatics or a handhold of everyone who enters.

#### Downgrading or Repurposing

A module may be deliberately downgraded or converted.

This usually requires:

- Deconstructing part of the existing system.
- Recovering some materials.
- Completing the new module’s Project.
- Recalibrating its power and controls.

A Medical Bay cannot become a Weapons Battery simply because both occupy one room. The crew must physically rebuild the space.

### Hull HP and Armor Plating

Hull HP represents the ship’s ability to survive structural damage without losing integrity.

Armor Plating represents the ship’s ability to prevent Hull Damage before it reaches the structure.

#### Suggested Hull and Armor Values

| Hull Tier | Maximum Hull HP | Armor Dice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Barely Standing | 4 | 0d6 |
| Patched | 6 | 1d6 |
| Reinforced | 8 | 3d6 |
| Plated | 10 | 5d6 |

These values may be adjusted for unusually small or large ships.

#### When the Ship Takes Damage

The ship only takes Hits from significant threats, such as:

- Collisions.
- Heavy weapons.
- Reactor explosions.
- Asteroid impacts.
- Severe gravitational stress.
- Neutron-star events.
- Catastrophic internal failures.
- Other hazards capable of damaging a vessel.

Minor accidents normally damage occupants, equipment, or individual modules rather than Hull HP.

#### Resolving a Hit

When the ship takes a Hit:

- Determine the incoming Hull Damage.
- Roll the ship’s current Armor Dice.
- Each 6 prevents 1 Hull Damage.
- Apply the remaining damage to Hull HP.
- Resolve any module or room effects caused by the Hit.

Armor Dice are rolled for every Hit and are not permanently spent.

#### Module Damage

A Hit may also damage a room or module when appropriate.

The GM should choose a module based on:

- The direction of the impact.
- The source of the damage.
- The ship’s layout.
- The current situation.
- Any targeting rules involved.

A normal Hit should not automatically deal full Hull Damage and destroy a module unless the threat is particularly severe.

### Critical Integrity

When Hull HP reaches 0, the ship enters Critical Integrity.

While at Critical Integrity:

- All Hull repair work becomes Crisis work.
- Ship movement becomes Crisis work.
- Expanding the ship is impossible.
- Every unprevented Hit causes a Catastrophic Event.
- A second unprevented Hit while still at Critical Integrity destroys the ship.

#### Catastrophic Events

Possible Catastrophic Events include:

- Massive decompression.
- Reactor breach.
- Total power loss.
- Destruction of a module.
- Fire spread through several rooms.
- A room being torn from the ship.
- Loss of engines.
- Loss of navigation.
- Cargo vented into space.
- Severe radiation flooding the ship.

The first Hit at Critical Integrity causes the Catastrophic Event.

If the ship suffers another unprevented Hit before Hull HP is restored above 0, the ship is destroyed.

Total ship destruction kills everyone aboard unless a specific escape or survival method is available.

### Repairing the Hull

Hull HP cannot normally be restored without an Operational Workshop or Fabrication Bay.

Repairing Hull HP uses a Ship Project.

Each Progress restores 1 Hull HP and consumes the listed materials.

Hull HP cannot exceed the ship’s current maximum.

#### Hull Repair

Skill: Mechanical Technician or Engineering

Routine Threshold: Mechanical Technician 3 or Engineering 4

Contributor Limit: 2

Energy per Contribution: 2

Facility: Operational Workshop

Toolkit: 1 charge per Progress

Progress: 1 Progress per Hull HP restored

#### Cost per Hull HP

##### Barely Standing or Patched Hull

- 3 Scrap Metal.
- 1 Basic Adhesive.
- 1 Toolkit charge.

##### Reinforced Hull

- 2 Reinforced Steel.
- 1 Basic Adhesive.
- 1 Toolkit charge.

##### Plated Hull

- 1 Neutronium.
- 1 Advanced Adhesive.
- 1 Toolkit charge.

#### Repairing at Critical Integrity

Hull repair while the ship is at Critical Integrity is always Crisis work.

On a failed repair contribution, the GM may introduce:

- Decompression.
- Loss of installed material.
- Injury to a worker.
- Damage to an adjacent module.
- Increased Pressure.
- A new structural fault.

Restoring even 1 Hull HP ends Critical Integrity.

Existing Catastrophic damage remains until separately repaired.

#### Improvised Hull Repairs

If the Workshop is unavailable, the crew may attempt an improvised external or internal patch.

An improvised Hull repair:

- Is always Skilled or Crisis.
- Suffers −3d6.
- Requires plausible materials and tools.
- Restores temporary Hull HP.

<!-- updated: labor -->

A character working with their shoulder instead of a toolkit may Brace the Hull: a Labor Task, always Skilled, with no −3d6 because it never pretended to be a repair. It restores 1 temporary Hull HP and nothing more.

Temporary Hull HP lasts until:

- The ship takes another Hit.
- The ship enters a hazardous tile.
- A severe Event occurs.
- The repair is replaced properly.

Temporary Hull HP cannot raise the ship above 1 Hull HP.

Repairing Modules

Each module lists its Repair Profile. As a general baseline:

| Repair | Typical Progress |
| --- | --- |
| Damaged to Operational | 1 Progress |
| Critical to Damaged | 2 Progress |
| Offline to Critical | 3 Progress |
| Destroyed module | Rebuild using its Construction Project |

A repair restores one condition step when the required Progress is completed.

### Example Ship Project: Building a Workshop

The crew wants to install a Grade I Workshop in an empty room.

The Workshop requires:

Skill: Engineering

Routine Threshold: Engineering 3

Progress Required: 4

Contributor Limit: 2

Energy per Contribution: 2

Toolkit: Improvised Toolkit or better

Upfront Materials: 1 Basic Adhesive

Materials per Progress: 2 Scrap Metal and 1 Scrap Component

Power Draw: 1 Power Unit when completed

The crew’s Engineer has Engineering 6 and meets the Routine Threshold.

The Quartermaster has Engineering 1.

During the Morning segment:

- The Engineer contributes Routine work and adds 1 Progress.
- Because the Engineer qualifies for Routine Benefits, they choose Project Efficiency and add 1 additional Progress.
- The Quartermaster attempts a Skilled contribution and succeeds, adding 1 Progress.

The Project reaches 3 of 4 Progress in one segment.

The required materials for all three Progress are consumed.

During the Afternoon, the Engineer adds the final Progress automatically.

The Workshop is complete.

It remains Inactive until the crew allocates 1 Power Unit to it.

Every hour spent improving the ship is an hour the crew may not have to spend desperately surviving tomorrow

## Chapter 5: Blood, Smoke, and What’s Left

Expeditions

Expeditions are the most dangerous thing you can do in Adrift.

They are where supplies are found, answers are uncovered, and people get hurt. Outside the ship, survival systems no longer protect you.

This chapter covers how expeditions work, how combat is handled, and how both interact with the survival systems defined earlier.

Food, medicine, replacement components, intact technology, and answers are all waiting outside the ship. Every expedition is a decision to leave functioning Life Support behind and enter a place that has already killed everyone who once depended on it.

This chapter explains:

- Preparing and departing for an expedition.
- Expedition time and exploration.
- Zones, movement, and line of sight.
- Stealth, detection, and surprise.
- Environmental hazards and Expedition Clocks.
- Initiative and combat rounds.
- Slow Actions, Fast Actions, and Reactions.
- Attacks, Armor, damage, and injuries.
- Pressure during immediate danger.
- Retreating and extracting wounded crew.
- Searching rooms and recovering salvage.
- Securing and stripping derelicts.
- Returning to the ship.

The general rules for skill rolls, Routine, Skilled, and Crisis Tasks, Pressure, Stress, HP, injuries, and death are described in Chapter 2.

Inventory, equipment repair, charges, and consumables are described in Chapter 3.

### Expeditions

An expedition begins when one or more characters leave the safety of their ship to enter a derelict, station, wreck, asteroid, colony, alien structure, or other hostile environment.

Expeditions are played more freely than ordinary shipboard segments. Players describe where they go, what they examine, and how they respond to danger.

Time, however, still matters.

Every sealed door opened, room searched, wound treated, and machine dismantled gives the surrounding environment more time to change.

#### The Expedition Procedure

An expedition generally follows this sequence:

- Select the expedition crew.
- Prepare equipment.
- Enter the site.
- Explore using Expedition Turns.
- Resolve hazards, discoveries, and combat.
- Decide when to withdraw.
- Return to the ship.
- Resolve Pressure, Stress, injuries, salvage, and elapsed time.

The crew does not need to complete every possible objective.

They may leave at any time, provided they can still reach an exit.

### Preparing for Departure

Before an expedition begins, complete the following checks.

#### Crew

Record which characters are leaving and which remain aboard.

Characters left aboard may perform shipboard Tasks during the same day segment unless events interfere.

#### Inventory

Every participating character must remain within their expedition inventory limit.

The inventory rules are described in Chapter 3. A crew that fills every slot before departure has no room to bring anything back. Pick and choose wisely.

### Expedition Time

Expeditions occur inside the same Morning, Afternoon, Evening, and Night segments used aboard the ship.

To track shorter periods of activity, expeditions use Expedition Turns.

#### Expedition Turns

One Expedition Turn represents approximately twenty to forty minutes of cautious movement, searching, technical work, negotiation, treatment, or other meaningful activity.

Eight Expedition Turns consume one day segment.

<!-- updated: expedition-turns -->

After the eighth Expedition Turn:

- Advance to the next day segment.
- Resolve any effects tied to that segment.
- Advance environmental and threat clocks.
- Consume supplies when required.
- Allow the crew to reconsider whether it continues or withdraws.

The GM may shorten or lengthen a Turn when the fiction clearly demands it. The purpose is to make time a visible cost, not to measure every minute.

#### Combat and Expedition Time

Combat uses rounds rather than Expedition Turns. Time does not progress normally during combat, unless the combat extends for an extraneously long period for whatever reason. Progression then will be up to the GM’s discretion.

After combat, activities such as treating injuries, searching bodies, repairing equipment, regrouping, or moving the wounded use Expedition Turns normally.

#### Splitting the Crew

Characters in separate locations still use the same Expedition Turn.

At the end of each Turn, all separated groups resolve their actions before time advances.

Splitting the crew allows more ground to be covered.

It also creates several smaller groups that may need help at the same time.

### Exploring a Site

At the beginning of each Expedition Turn:

- The GM describes all immediately visible conditions.
- The crew decides whether it remains together or separates.
- Each group may move to one adjacent accessible zone.
- Each character may perform one Expedition Task.
- Resolve all Tasks and consequences.
- Advance relevant Expedition Clocks.
- Mark one Expedition Turn.

Ordinary movement inside the same secure zone does not use a Task.

Moving through a dangerous border, crossing several zones, carrying something Heavy, or moving while under pressure may require a Task.

#### Common Expedition Tasks

##### Scout Ahead

Skill: Perception or Stealth

Purpose: Examine the next zone without exposing the entire crew.

A success may reveal:

- Creatures.
- Traps.
- Environmental hazards.
- Exits.
- Cover.
- Obvious salvage.
- Signs of recent movement.

A failure may expose the scout, miss a danger, or create surprise.

##### Search

Skill: Scavenging

Purpose: Search a zone for concealed or valuable supplies.

Search rules are described later in this chapter.

##### Analyze

Skill: Biology, Engineering, Navigation, or another appropriate Skill

Purpose: Understand an organism, machine, signal, structure, or hazard.

Unknown alien subjects are normally Skilled or Crisis.

##### Breach

Skill: Mechanical Technician, Engineering, Close Combat, Heavy Weaponry, or Labor

Purpose: Open a sealed door, cut through wreckage, or force an obstructed route.

As a Labor Task this uses Strength against the Labor Threshold the GM sets for the obstruction, normally 2 for something stuck and 3 or more for something that was meant to stay shut.

Breaching is usually loud.

##### Secure

Skill: Engineering, Mechanical Technician, or another appropriate Skill

Purpose: Seal doors, disable traps, restore atmosphere, anchor equipment, or make a zone safer.

##### Field Repair

Skill: Mechanical Technician or Engineering

Purpose: Restore damaged portable equipment or create a temporary repair.

Repairs during immediate danger are Crisis Tasks.

##### Treat Injury

Skill: First Aid

Purpose: Stop Bleeding, stabilize a character, or treat accessible Minor injuries.

Stopping Bleeding is always Crisis.

##### Stand Watch

Skill: Perception

Purpose: Protect others while they work.

A character Standing Watch may detect approaching danger before it creates surprise.

##### Carry or Extract

Skill: Labor, or Acrobatics when the route matters more than the weight

Purpose: Move a downed character, Heavy object, specimen, or piece of machinery.

As a Labor Task this uses Strength against the Labor Threshold the GM sets for the load, normally 2 for an awkward one and 3 or more for something that should take two people.

##### Catch Your Breath

Requirement: A secured zone with no immediate threat

Spend one Expedition Turn to reduce Pressure by 1.

Catching Your Breath is not Rest.

It does not restore Energy, satisfy daily sleep requirements, or enable natural injury recovery.

And whatever other actions the party would like to do.

### Zones

Expedition locations are divided into zones.

A zone is a distinct area in which characters can move and interact without tracking exact distance.

A zone may be:

- A room.
- A corridor.
- A cargo bay.
- A bridge.
- A laboratory.
- A section of open terrain.
- A maintenance shaft.
- Part of a larger chamber.
- A drifting section of wreckage.

A zone is usually between a few steps and approximately twenty-five meters across.

Tight interiors use smaller zones. Open environments use larger ones.

#### Adjacent Zones

Two zones are adjacent when a character could move directly between them. The map or GM should make adjacency clear.

#### Borders

A border between zones may be:

- Open.
- Closed.
- Locked.
- Sealed.
- Blocked.
- Hazardous.
- Destroyed.

An open border permits movement and line of sight.

A blocked border prevents movement until the obstacle is bypassed, removed, or breached.

#### Doors and Hatches

A closed door normally blocks line of sight.

Opening an ordinary unlocked door is free during exploration and a Fast Action during combat.

A locked, sealed, damaged, or pressurized door may require a Task.

#### Line of Sight

A character has line of sight when no solid border or major obstruction blocks vision.

Characters standing at an open doorway may see into the adjoining zone.

Darkness, smoke, debris, steam, biological growth, and environmental interference may limit line of sight without completely blocking it.

### Cover and Position

Position matters even without exact grid measurements.

#### Partial Cover

Partial Cover protects part of the body.

Examples include:

- A doorway.
- A control console.
- Machinery.
- A low wall.
- Dense wreckage.

Partial Cover provides 1 Cover Armor against Ballistic attacks.

#### Strong Cover

Strong Cover protects most of the body.

Examples include:

- A reinforced bulkhead opening.
- Heavy machinery.
- A defensive barricade.
- A narrow firing slit.

Strong Cover provides 2 Cover Armor against Ballistic attacks.

Cover Armor does not normally degrade when used.

An attack stunt, Heavy Weapon, explosion, or environmental effect may damage or destroy the cover.

#### Full Cover

Full Cover completely blocks line of sight.

A character behind Full Cover cannot normally be directly targeted.

Area effects, breaching weapons, environmental damage, and attacks from another direction may still reach them.

### Expedition Clocks

An Expedition Clock represents a danger, opportunity, or environmental change that advances while the crew acts.

Clocks normally contain four or six boxes.

Examples include:

- Patrol Arrives.
- Reactor Meltdown.
- Oxygen Failure.
- Derelict Collapse.
- Fire Spreads.
- Signal Lost.
- Specimen Awakens.
- Hunter Finds the Crew.
- Neutron Pulse.
- Survivors Suffocate.
- Escape Route Closes.

#### Advancing a Clock

A clock may advance when:

- An Expedition Turn ends.
- A loud action is taken.
- A relevant Task fails.
- A system remains unrepaired.
- The crew enters a particular zone.
- The fiction otherwise demands it.

The GM will state visible clocks whenever the crew could reasonably understand the danger. Hidden clocks may be used for threats the crew has not detected.

#### Filling a Clock

When the final box is filled, its event occurs immediately.

Examples include:

- Enemies enter the map.
- A route becomes blocked.
- The atmosphere begins venting.
- A room becomes radioactive.
- The derelict changes course.
- A surviving machine activates.
- An opportunity disappears.

### Stealth and Detection

Stealth is used when a character or group attempts to avoid detection.

#### Individual Stealth

A single character makes a normal Stealth roll when the outcome is uncertain.

One success avoids ordinary detection.

Additional successes may:

- Conceal evidence of passage.
- Move into a better position.
- Hide another nearby character.
- Learn something about the observer.
- Avoid advancing a Noise Clock.

#### Group Stealth

When the crew moves together, one character leads the attempt.

Use the leader’s Stealth roll.

Apply:

- −1d6 for each character after the first with Stealth 0.
- −1d6 for each character carrying Bulky or noisy equipment.
- A maximum total group penalty of −3d6.

One character may Assist normally.

A failure exposes the group or advances an appropriate Clock.

#### Ordinary Detection

A character Standing Watch with Perception 2 or higher automatically detects obvious approaching threats in a secure, well-lit zone.

Detection becomes Skilled when the threat is:

- Hidden.
- Silent.
- Camouflaged.
- Behind cover.
- Moving through darkness.
- Biologically or technologically concealed.
- Approaching through an unexpected route.

### Surprise

Surprise occurs when one side begins combat without detecting the other.

<!-- updated: surprised -->

Surprised characters:

- Become Shaken, if not already under more Pressure.
- Cannot take a Reaction or Fast Action until the end of their first turn.
- Draw one Initiative card normally.

Characters participating in a successful ambush:

- Take one free slow action.
- Draw two Initiative cards, and keep the higher card.
- May begin behind appropriate Cover if the fiction allows it.

After Initiative is drawn, combat proceeds normally.

## Chapter 6: Teeth in the Dark

Combat Mechanics

Eventually, something notices you.

A firefight may rupture a hull, ignite the atmosphere, draw something from deeper inside the wreck, or destroy the supplies the crew came to recover. Even a victory may leave someone Bleeding, contaminated, out of ammunition, and too injured to reach the ship. To make it out of this dead system alive, you’ll have to put down the threats that await.

### Conditions

Conditions are temporary or persistent states that change what a character or creature can do. Taking damage does not automatically inflict a condition unless the attack, weapon, ability, or stunt says otherwise.

Conditions may affect players, enemies, machines, and other targets. A condition only applies when it makes sense for the affected target. A machine cannot normally become Poisoned, while a creature without electronic components cannot normally become Disrupted.

#### Condition Duration

Conditions use one of the following durations.

##### Brief

The condition ends at the end of the affected target’s next turn.

##### Ongoing

The condition remains until the target performs the listed action, leaves the source, or meets another stated requirement.

##### Scene

The condition lasts until the current combat, immediate danger, or expedition encounter ends.

##### Persistent

The condition remains after the current scene and requires treatment, repair, decontamination, or another substantial solution.

The source of a condition may specify a different duration.

#### Reapplying Conditions

Conditions do not normally stack with themselves.

Applying the same condition again refreshes its duration.

Some conditions have an escalation effect. When such a condition is applied again, use the escalation described in its entry instead of merely refreshing it.

Different conditions may affect the same target simultaneously.

#### Pressure States

Calm, Shaken, Pressured, Overtaxed, and Panicked are Pressure states, not ordinary conditions.

They follow the Pressure rules in Chapter 2 and are not removed by abilities that remove conditions unless those abilities explicitly affect Pressure.

### Entering Combat

Combat begins when precise action order matters.

Common triggers include:

- A character attacks.
- A creature charges.
- A trap begins acting repeatedly.
- Two sides attempt incompatible actions at the same time.
- Immediate movement determines who survives.
- A hazard begins resolving every few seconds.

Once combat begins:

- Determine surprise.
- Draw Initiative.
- Establish each participant’s zone and position.
- Begin the first Round.

### Initiative

Use ten Initiative cards numbered 1 through 10.

Each participating player draws one card.

The GM draws one card for each important NPC or enemy group.

The lowest number acts first.

After the lowest card acts, proceed upward until every participant has taken a turn.

When all participants have acted, the Round ends and a new Round begins.

Initiative remains the same throughout the conflict unless an ability, stunt, or effect changes it.

#### Groups of Enemies

Several similar minor enemies may share one Initiative card.

They act together when that card is reached.

Important enemies will normally draw separately.

#### Trading Initiative

Before the first Round begins, two allied characters who can communicate may trade Initiative cards.

After combat begins, Initiative may only be changed by a stunt or ability.

### Combat Turns

On their turn, a character may perform:

- One Slow Action and one Fast Action, in either order.
- Two Fast Actions.

A character may also speak briefly without spending an Action.

Long explanations, negotiation, technical instructions, or complex coordination may require an Action when time is critical.

#### Slow Actions

Slow Actions usually involve an attack, a skill roll, or sustained work.

Common Slow Actions include:

<!-- updated: combat-actions -->

- Close Combat Attack.
- Shoot Firearm.
- Burst Fire: needs an automatic weapon and uses extra ammunition. The attack gains +2d6, and extra 6s can be split between targets in the same zone.
- Grapple: win a contested Close Combat or Strength roll to make an adjacent target Grappled.
- Throw Weapon or Object.
- Reload.
- Sprint.
- Crawl while prone.
- Overwatch.
- Stop Bleeding.
- Perform First Aid.
- Use complex equipment.
- Breach or seal a door.
- Repair a system.
- Drag a downed character.
- Perform another meaningful Task.
- Use a special ability to your discretion
- Mark an enemy: a flat Perception roll. On a 6, the target is Marked.
- Destroy a discarded or Disarmed weapon.

#### Fast Actions

Fast Actions are brief movements or interactions.

Common Fast Actions include:

<!-- updated: combat-actions -->

- Move one zone.
- Get up.
- Drop prone.
- Draw or stow an item.
- Pick up an accessible item.
- Open or close an ordinary door.
- Aim: +1d6 to your next ranged attack this turn.
- Take Cover.
- Disengage.
- Extinguish yourself.
- Reserve a Reaction.
- Activate simple equipment.
- Drop an item.
- Use a special ability to your discretion

The GM may classify other actions according to their complexity.

### Movement in Combat

#### Move

A Fast Action moves a character into one adjacent zone.

The border must be accessible.

#### Sprint

A Slow Action moves a character up to two zones.

Sprint cannot cross a border requiring a Task.

#### Difficult Terrain

Moving through rubble, zero-gravity debris, deep fluid, unstable surfaces, or another hazard may require Acrobatics.

On failure, the character:

- Does not complete the movement.
- Falls prone.
- Drifts into danger.
- Advances a hazard Clock.
- Suffers another stated consequence.

#### Prone

A prone character:

- Must spend a Fast Action to stand.
- Suffers −2d6 to Close Combat attacks.
- Gains Partial Cover against distant Ballistic attacks when the environment permits.
- Moves only by using the Crawl Slow Action.

#### Engaged Characters

Characters in the same zone as a hostile creature are Engaged.

Leaving an Engaged zone without using Disengage allows one hostile creature in that zone to make an immediate Close Combat Attack.

A character may only make one such free attack per Round.

#### Disengage

Spend a Fast Action before moving.

The character may leave an Engaged zone without triggering a free attack.

### Attacks

To attack:

- Choose a valid target.
- Confirm range and line of sight.
- Spend any ammunition or charge.
- Roll the relevant Skill.
- Apply Pressure Dice and modifiers.
- Resolve success, Reactions, damage, and stunts.

#### Relevant Skills

- Close Combat: Melee weapons and unarmed attacks.
- Light Firearms: Pistols, rifles, and personal firearms.
- Heavy Weaponry: Heavy guns, explosives, mounted weapons, and certain ship weapons.

Other weapons may list a different Skill.

#### Successful Attacks

One success hits the target.

The attack deals the weapon’s listed Damage.

Each additional success may be spent on an appropriate combat stunt.

### Range

Weapons list their effective range in zones.

General range categories are:

| Range | Distance |
| --- | --- |
| Close | Same zone |
| Short | Adjacent zone |
| Medium | Up to 2 zones |
| Long | Up to 4 zones |

A weapon may attack one zone beyond its listed range at −3d6.

Attacks beyond that are normally impossible.

Blocked line of sight still prevents an attack regardless of range.

### Reactions

A character may reserve one Fast Action as a Reaction.

A character may use only one Reaction per Round.

An unused Reaction is lost at the beginning of the character’s next turn.

#### Block

Use when targeted by a Close Combat Attack.

Roll Close Combat.

One success prevents the attack.

Additional successes may:

- Disarm the attacker.
- Push the attacker away.
- Knock the attacker prone.
- Gain a positional advantage.

A character cannot Block an attack they could not perceive.

#### Dodge

Use when targeted by a visible ranged attack, thrown weapon, or avoidable area effect.

Roll Acrobatics.

One success causes the attack to miss that character.

The character must have somewhere plausible to move, duck, or throw themselves.

A character behind Full Cover cannot Dodge because they are not a valid direct target in the first place.

#### Reactive Movement

Some abilities or equipment may permit movement as a Reaction.

Unless explicitly stated, ordinary characters cannot move as a Reaction.

### Overwatch

Overwatch is a Slow Action.

Choose:

- One visible zone.
- One doorway or border.
- One specific trigger.

Examples include:

- “When something enters that corridor.”
- “When the creature leaves cover.”
- “When the hatch opens.”

Before the character’s next turn, they may make one ranged attack when the trigger occurs.

The attack interrupts the triggering action.

Overwatch ends when:

- The attack is made.
- The character moves.
- The character takes HP damage.
- The character loses line of sight.
- The character’s next turn begins.

### Throwing Weapons and Objects

Throwing an object is a Slow Action.

Use:

- Close Combat for balanced throwing weapons.
- Heavy Weaponry for grenades and explosives.
- Strength for crude or improvised objects when appropriate.

Throwing into the same or an adjacent zone has no range penalty.

Throwing two zones away suffers −3d6 unless the item is designed for it.

A failed explosive throw may scatter, land in the wrong zone, fail to activate, or create another immediate complication.

### Damage

Weapons and hazards deal a fixed amount of Damage.

<!-- updated: mech-damage, injury-threshold, damage-pressure -->

Apply Damage in this order: Vulnerability, then Resistance, then Cover (against Ballistic Damage only), then worn Armor. Whatever remains reduces HP. The thresholds below count HP actually lost.

If at least 2 HP is lost from one source:

- Gain 1 Pressure, no more than once per Round from taking damage.

If a single hit takes 3 or more HP, or the character drops to 0 HP:

- Roll for an Injury using Chapter 2.

After any HP loss:

- Apply any damage-type effect.
- Check whether the character reaches 0 HP.

Damage cannot normally reduce HP below 0.

<!-- updated: resistance -->

#### Resistance and Vulnerability

Resistance to a damage type halves each hit of that type, rounded down (3 becomes 1). Vulnerability adds half, rounded down (3 becomes 4). Resistance has no levels: any source gives it, and more sources don't stack. Resistance and Vulnerability to the same type cancel out. Immunity means that type deals no damage.

The short-lived Vulnerable: Damage Type condition is separate: +1 damage on the next hit of that type.

### Armor

Armor in Adrift is ablative.

It keeps a character alive by being destroyed in their place.

Armor has an Armor value.

Whenever an attack would deal HP damage and the Armor protects against that damage type:

- Reduce the incoming Damage by 1.
- Reduce the Armor value by 1.
- Apply the remaining Damage to HP.

Example:

A character with 3 Armor is hit for 3 Ballistic Damage.

The Armor reduces the Damage by 1.

The character takes 2 HP Damage.

Their Armor is reduced from 3 to 2.

Armor at 0 provides no protection until repaired.

#### Armor and Cover

Apply Cover Armor before worn Armor.

Cover Armor does not normally degrade.

#### Armor-Piercing Attacks

<!-- updated: armor-piercing -->

Armor Piercing X ignores the first X points of protection: Cover first, then worn Armor.

An attack with Armor Piercing 1 ignores the first point of protection.

#### Sealed Armor

Sealed Armor protects against vacuum, Chemical hazards, and biological contamination only while its seals remain functional.

Losing ordinary Armor does not automatically break the seal.

Specific damage, stunts, or hazards may cause a suit breach.

### Pressure in Combat

Pressure functions as described in Chapter 2.

Pressure Dice increase the character’s chance of success, but failed rolls become increasingly destructive.

<!-- updated: pressure-table -->

| Pressure State | Pressure Dice | Failure Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calm | +0d6 | No additional consequence |
| Shaken | +1d6 | Become Pressured |
| Pressured | +2d6 | Lose 1 current Energy and gain 1 Stress |
| Overtaxed | +3d6 | Lose all current Energy and gain 1 Pressure and 1 Stress |
| Panicked | +4d6 | Lose all current Energy, suffer 2 HP Damage, gain 1 Stress, and trigger a Mind Break |

Combat does not normally spend Energy.

Pressure failures can still reduce Energy and affect what the character can do after the fight.

#### After Combat

When immediate danger clearly ends, reduce each surviving character’s Pressure by 1.

Pressure does not automatically return to Calm merely because Initiative has ended.

If the crew remains trapped, hunted, exposed, or unable to secure the area, the immediate danger may not have ended.

A character who reaches Panicked during an expedition gains 1 Stress when the expedition ends, to a maximum of once per expedition.

<!-- updated: expedition-reset -->

Abilities usable once per expedition reset when the expedition ends.

### Downed Characters

A character at 0 HP is down.

They are:

- Prone.
- Unable to perform normal Actions.
- Vulnerable to further harm.
- Required to make death checks as described in Chapter 2.

At the end of each Round, make a flat Constitution roll.

On failure, the character dies.

One Willpower may reroll the failed check.

#### Moving a Downed Character

Dragging a downed character is a Slow Action.

The rescuer may move one zone.

Two characters carrying together may move normally with one Fast Action each.

Bulky equipment, low gravity, tight spaces, or severe injury may change this.

#### Stabilizing Bleeding

<!-- updated: bleeding, injury-severity -->

Stopping Bleeding is a Slow Action. Bleeding ends with a successful First Aid task or by spending an appropriate Medkit use.

On success:

- Bleeding ends.
- The character remains at their current HP.

On failure:

- One Medkit use is consumed.
- Bleeding continues.
- Apply the acting character’s Pressure failure consequence.

### Retreat and Withdrawal

Combat does not need to end with one side dead.

Creatures may flee, surrender, hide, withdraw, or abandon their objective.

The crew may do the same.

#### Individual Withdrawal

A character may leave combat by reaching a secure exit zone.

Movement and Disengage rules still apply.

#### Pursuit

Enemies may pursue beyond the current combat map.

Resolve pursuit using:

- Expedition movement.
- Stealth.
- Acrobatics.
- Piloting.
- An appropriate Clock.

Combat may begin again if the pursuers catch the crew.

### Ending Combat

Combat ends when:

- All hostile participants are dead, downed, restrained, or gone.
- One side successfully withdraws.
- The conflict’s objective is resolved.
- Both sides agree to stop.
- The environment makes continued combat impossible.

Do not keep Initiative active when exact action order no longer matters.

### Securing a Zone

A zone is Secured when:

- No active hostile creature controls it.
- Immediate traps and hazards are identified or neutralized.
- The crew controls its accessible entrances.
- The environment is stable enough for ordinary work.

A zone may become unsecured later.

#### Securing a Derelict

A derelict is Secured when:

- The Primary hostile force has been defeated, contained, bypassed, or driven away.
- A stable route exists between the ship and the relevant salvage areas.

A Secured derelict may still contain:

- Locked rooms.
- Environmental hazards.
- Hidden threats.
- Unexplored sections.
- Story discoveries.

The crew may return to those areas through another expedition.

#### Search Task

Skill: Scavenging

Time: One Expedition Turn

Normal Category: Skilled

On one success, find one relevant salvage result or hidden feature.

Each additional success may:

- Find another salvage result.
- Improve the material tier.
- Locate a unique item.
- Avoid a trap or contamination.
- Search quietly.
- Reduce the time cost of recovery.
- Identify where more salvage is located.

A failed Search:

- Consumes the Expedition Turn.
- Finds nothing beyond the obvious.
- May advance a Threat or Noise Clock when appropriate.

A zone normally permits one standard Search.

Searching it again requires:

- New information.
- Specialized equipment.
- A Thorough Search.
- A significant change in the environment.

#### Thorough Search

A Thorough Search consumes two Expedition Turns and grants +3d6.

It always advances the site’s Noise or Threat Clock by 1 when one exists.

A Thorough Search may reveal hidden compartments, rare materials, concealed survivors, structural routes, or dangerous secrets.

### Salvage Capacity

Each significant expedition site has a Salvage Capacity.

Salvage Capacity represents the number of useful material bundles the site still contains.

| Site | Salvage Capacity |
| --- | --- |
| Small room or wreckage pocket | 1–2 |
| Minor derelict | 3–4 |
| Large derelict or station section | 5–8 |
| Major story location | Determined by the GM |

Each successful salvage recovery removes 1 Salvage Capacity.

When Capacity reaches 0, the site has been stripped of ordinary useful material.

Unique story items do not necessarily use Salvage Capacity.

#### Salvage Bundles

One Salvage bundle normally contains one of the following:

- 2 Scrap Metal.
- 2 Scrap Components.
- 1 Basic Adhesive.
- 1 Chemical.
- 1 Medical Supply.
- 1 Food Unit.
- 1 Water Unit.
- Another comparable amount appropriate to the site.

Higher-tier sites may produce better bundles.

### Returning to the Ship

An expedition ends when all surviving participants have returned to the ship or another established safe location.

Complete the following procedure:

<!-- updated: expedition-reset -->

- Record elapsed Expedition Turns and day segments.
- Transfer recovered items and salvage into ship storage.
- Record abandoned or destroyed equipment.
- Resolve active Bleeding, contamination, fire, or suit breaches.
- Reduce Pressure by 1 if immediate danger has ended.
- Gain 1 Stress for reaching Panicked during the expedition, maximum once.
- Reset once-per-expedition abilities.
- Consume any required Food, Water, Oxygen, Fuel, or medical supplies.
- Update the site’s Salvage Capacity and condition.
- Decide whether the site is Secured, abandoned, or still active.

### Physical and Ballistic Conditions

These conditions are commonly caused by firearms, blades, crushing attacks, claws, shrapnel, and other kinetic trauma.

#### Bleeding

The target takes 1 unblockable Physical Damage each turn.

Bleeding can trigger only once per Round, and Bleeding is persistent until treated.

A Bleeding target that remains still and performs no Slow Actions does not take this damage.

Bleeding is removed by:

- A successful First Aid Task.
- Spending an appropriate Medkit use.
- A biological or cybernetic ability that seals wounds.
- Another effect that explicitly stops Bleeding.

A target already Bleeding that gains Bleeding again immediately takes 1 Physical Damage.

#### Brittle

The target’s structure, shell, armor, or bones have been weakened.

The next Physical or Ballistic hit against the target:

- Deals +1 Damage.
- Damages one additional point of Armor if the attack already damages Armor.

Brittle ends after triggering or at the end of the Scene.

#### Crippled

One limb or major movement structure has been seriously damaged.

When Crippled is applied, the source states what was affected.

Examples include:

- Arm: −3d6 to Tasks requiring that arm.
- Leg: The target becomes Slowed and cannot Sprint.
- Wing: The target cannot fly normally.
- Tail or stabilizer: The target suffers −2d6 to balance and rapid movement.
- Weapon limb: One natural attack becomes unusable.

Crippled is normally Persistent and may require Surgery, repair, regeneration, or another substantial treatment.

#### Disarmed

The target drops one held item chosen by the effect’s source.

The item lands within the target’s zone.

Recovering it normally requires a Fast Action.

Disarmed is resolved immediately rather than remaining active.

### Fire Conditions

Fire conditions represent heat, ignition, smoke, and thermal exposure.

#### Burning

The target takes 1 Fire Damage at the start of each turn. Burning is Ongoing.

It may be removed by:

- Spending a Fast Action to extinguish the flames.
- An adjacent character spending a Fast Action to help.
- Entering water, vacuum, fire-suppression foam, or another environment that smothers the flames.
- Using appropriate equipment.

A Burning target that becomes Burning again immediately takes 1 Fire Damage. Burning may ignite vulnerable objects and environments.

#### Smoldering

The target emits visible smoke, heat, sparks, or embers.

While :

- The target cannot Hide.
- Heat-sensitive creatures and sensors automatically detect its general location.
- The next source of Fire Damage also applies Burning.

Smoldering normally lasts until the end of the Scene or until the target spends a Fast Action cooling or cleaning itself.

#### Illuminated

The target or zone produces enough light to be clearly visible.

An Illuminated target:

- Cannot benefit from darkness.
- Cannot Hide using visual concealment alone.
- May trigger special weaknesses in light-sensitive creatures.

Illuminated normally lasts until the light source is removed or the Scene ends.

#### Overheated

<!-- updated: machine-conditions -->

The target’s body, armor, cybernetics, or machinery has exceeded safe temperatures.

While Overheated:

- The target suffers −2d6 to strenuous physical Tasks.
- It cannot safely use abilities that would produce additional heat.
- It cannot be overdriven, pushed past its rating, or Redlined again until it cools.

A character, mech, or other target that acts for itself ends Overheated by spending a Slow Action cooling down in an appropriate environment.

A ship module, facility, or installation takes no actions of its own, so it cannot cool itself. Instead:

- Tasks performed with it or in it suffer −2d6.
- It still functions, still draws power, and still counts as its own Grade.
- It cools on its own at End of Day.

Overheated is the mildest of the three machine states. Nothing is broken; it is simply too hot to lean on, and the only cost is waiting.

### Electric Conditions

Electric conditions interfere with movement, nervous systems, machinery, and electronic equipment.

#### Conductive

The target has become unusually vulnerable to electrical transfer.

The next Electric hit against it:

- Deals +1 Damage.
- Applies Staggered without requiring a stunt.

Conductive ends after triggering or at the end of the Scene.

A target may become Conductive through:

- Water.
- Exposed wiring.
- Conductive chemicals.
- Biological fluids.
- Specialized equipment.
- Damage to insulating armor.

#### Disrupted

Electronic systems and powered equipment temporarily stop functioning.

While Disrupted:

- Activated cybernetic abilities cannot be used.
- Equipment-based Reactions cannot be used.
- Targeting systems, powered tools, and electronic weapons may become unavailable.
- Machines may lose one Fast Action or equivalent function.

A biological target without electronic components is normally immune.

Disrupted is removed by:

- Spending a Fast Action rebooting in a secure situation.
- Completing a Skilled repair or Engineering Task during danger.
- Waiting until the end of the target’s next turn, if the source gives it a Brief duration.

#### Jammed

The target cannot use remote communication, wireless systems, targeting links, or electronic coordination.

Jammed may affect:

- Radios.
- Drones.
- Remote weapons.
- Sensor feeds.
- Mech targeting.
- Shipboard communication.

Direct physical controls continue to function unless the source states otherwise.

Jammed ends after leaving the affected area or disabling the source.

#### Power-Starved

<!-- updated: machine-conditions -->

The target has been cut off from the power it needs: a tripped coupling, a drained reserve, a system that shut itself down rather than tear itself apart. Nothing is broken. It simply isn’t running.

Power-Starved applies to mechs, ship modules, vehicles, consoles, and any other machine. A purely biological target cannot be Power-Starved.

While Power-Starved:

- The target provides no function, benefit, or output of any kind. Treat a module as though it were not aboard.
- It draws no power. A Power-Starved ship module does not count against the grid.
- Powered modules, active abilities, and anything that runs off it are unavailable.
- A Power-Starved mech additionally loses its Fast Action, and a character may need a Skilled Task to climb out of one.

Power-Starved ends when someone restarts it: a Slow Action aboard ship or in a Scene, or one Task on the schedule, rolling Engineering or Mechanical Technician as a Skilled Task. **No materials, Progress, or toolkit charges are spent.** It is also removed outright if proper power is restored to it by any other means.

Power-Starved sits between Overheated and Damaged. It costs somebody’s action to clear, but nothing out of the hold.

### Chemical Conditions

Chemical conditions represent toxins, acids, corrosive compounds, gases, and biological contaminants.

#### Corroded

The target’s armor or protective surface has been chemically weakened.

The next time Armor would reduce damage:

- That Armor provides no damage reduction.
- The Armor loses 1 additional point.

Corroded then ends.

Corroded may also affect:

- Cover.
- Doors.
- Mechanical plating.
- Natural shells.
- Environmental seals.

A target without Armor may still be Corroded if the source specifies another effect.

#### Poisoned

The target suffers:

- −2d6 to Strength-based Tasks.
- −2d6 to Constitution-based Tasks.

During combat, the source may instead cause damage after a listed number of Rounds.

Poisoned is Persistent until removed through:

- An antidote.
- Medical treatment.
- Purging the toxin.
- An appropriate biological ability.
- Another listed solution.

Different poisons may produce additional effects.

#### Contaminated

Contamination may spread through:

- Physical contact.
- Shared atmosphere.
- Bodily fluids.
- Damaged suits.
- Improper storage.
- Contaminated equipment.

Contaminated is Persistent until the target undergoes appropriate decontamination.

Some contamination may also cause Poisoned, Stress, or mutation.

### Environmental Conditions

These conditions are commonly caused by vacuum, hazardous terrain, darkness, atmospheric failure, and expedition environments.

#### Suffocating

The target cannot breathe properly.

At the end of each Round, the target gains 1 Pressure.

After a number of Rounds determined by the environment, the target also begins taking Physical Damage.

Suffocating ends when breathable air is restored.

#### Freezing

The target suffers:

- −2d6 to Acrobatics and fine-motor Tasks.
- Slowed.
- Inability to recover from certain injuries or conditions.

Prolonged exposure may cause Physical Damage or an Injury.

Freezing ends after sufficient warmth is restored.

#### Anchored

The target cannot be forcibly moved, knocked Prone, pulled, or displaced against its will. Exceptions apply if environmental or other conditions are overwhelming(for example, if a whole is blown into the ship where the anchored individual is attached, they won’t be able to anchor themselves in the middle of space).

Anchored does not prevent voluntary movement unless the source states otherwise.

#### Weightless

The target is in zero gravity or otherwise lacks stable footing.

While Weightless:

- Ordinary movement may require Acrobatics or a handhold.
- Forced movement continues until something stops it.
- Prone normally has no effect.
- Recoil and impacts may move the target.

Weightless remains until gravity or another stabilizing force is restored.

### Behavioral and Mental Conditions

These conditions alter choices and behavior without replacing the Pressure system.

#### Enraged

The target gains +2d6 to Physical attacks.

While Enraged, it:

- Cannot use defensive Reactions.
- Cannot willingly retreat.
- Must move toward or attack an obvious threat when able.

Enraged normally lasts until the end of the Scene or until the source is removed.

#### Compelled

The target is driven toward a specific action stated by the source. They will prioritize over anything else, including their own safety.

Examples include:

- Protect a creature.
- Approach a sound.
- Attack the nearest target.
- Flee from light.
- Guard a location.
- Consume a substance.

A Compelled target may resist using the method described by the source.

#### Silenced

The target cannot speak, shout, or use voice-activated equipment.

While Silenced, the target cannot:

- Provide verbal Assistance.
- Issue spoken commands.
- Warn distant allies through speech.
- Use abilities requiring a voice.

Silenced ends when the source is removed.

#### Confused

At the beginning of the target’s turn, it must choose one:

- Lose one Fast Action while regaining focus.
- Suffer −3d6 to all Tasks during the turn.

Confused normally lasts until the end of the target’s next turn.

### Beneficial Conditions

Some conditions represent advantages rather than impairments.

Players and monsters may deliberately apply these conditions.

#### Regenerating

The target recovers a set amount of HP at the end of each turn.

The source states what suppresses regeneration.

Common suppressors include:

- Fire Damage.
- Chemical Damage.
- A specific atmosphere.
- Bright light.
- Destruction of a biological organ.
- A particular condition.

Regenerating does not restore destroyed limbs or remove Injuries unless explicitly stated.

#### Hidden

The target cannot be directly targeted by creatures that have not detected it.

Hidden ends when the target:

- Makes an obvious attack.
- Enters clear sight.
- Produces detectable noise.
- Is revealed by Perception or equipment.
- Performs another action the GM determines would expose it.

Knowing a Hidden target’s general location does not automatically reveal it.

#### Fortified

The target gains 1 temporary Armor against the next damaging hit.

This Armor:

- Is lost after reducing damage once.
- Does not require repair.
- Does not stack with itself unless stated otherwise.

#### Accelerated

The target gains one additional Fast Action during its next turn.

A target cannot normally benefit from Accelerated more than once per Round.

### Damage-Type Vulnerability

Some abilities apply temporary vulnerability as a condition.

#### Vulnerable: Damage Type

The next hit of the listed damage type deals +1 Damage.

Examples include:

- Vulnerable: Fire.
- Vulnerable: Electric.
- Vulnerable: Chemical.
- Vulnerable: Physical.
- Vulnerable: Ballistic.

The condition ends after triggering or at the end of the Scene.

This condition is separate from a creature’s permanent damage Vulnerability.

A target with both a permanent Vulnerability and Vulnerable: Fire takes both bonuses unless an ability states otherwise.

### Adaptive Defenses

Some monsters and upgrades change their defenses during an encounter.

#### Adaptive: Damage Type

The target gains Resistance 1 against the listed damage type.

If Adaptive is applied for a different damage type, the previous Adaptive Resistance is replaced.

For example, a creature with Adaptive: Ballistic that later suffers Fire Damage may change to Adaptive: Fire.

The creature’s ability determines when adaptation occurs.

Adaptive normally lasts until the end of the Scene.

### Resistance and Immunity to Conditions

Condition resistances are separate from damage resistances.

A creature may resist Electric Damage while remaining vulnerable to Staggered. It may also be immune to Staggered without resisting Electric Damage.

There are three levels of condition defense.

#### Normal

The condition applies normally.

#### Resistant

A Resistant target receives one of the following benefits, as stated in its profile:

- The condition’s duration is reduced by one category.
- The target gains +3d6 to Tasks made to remove the condition.
- The condition’s numerical effect is reduced.

If a player has Resistance to a condition, they can choose one of the following to apply to themselves.

For example:

Stagger Resistant: Applying Staggered requires one additional success.

Or:

Poison Resistant: The target gains +3d6 to resist and remove Poisoned.

#### Immune

The condition cannot affect the target.

### Removing Conditions

An effect that removes a condition must state what kinds of conditions it can remove.

Examples include:

- First Aid: Bleeding or certain Physical conditions.
- Decontamination: Contaminated and some Chemical conditions.
- Repair: Disrupted, Jammed, or mechanical conditions.
- Fire suppression: Burning and Smoldering.
- Willpower: Certain Behavioral conditions.
- Surgery: Crippled or severe Persistent conditions.
- Rest: Only conditions that explicitly end through Rest.

A general condition-removal effect does not normally remove:

- Injuries.
- Pressure.
- Stress.
- Afflictions.
- Permanent mutations.
- Destroyed equipment.

These systems require their own treatment.

### Unknown Conditions

New monsters, weapons, upgrades, and environments may create conditions beyond this list.

The Human Century

### Adrift Lore Expanded

Humanity’s expansion beyond Earth lasted only one hundred years.

In that time, people established orbital cities, crossed between stars, began transforming hostile planets, created entirely new ecosystems, and fought a final war they did not survive.

There were no ancient human empires among the stars. There were no thousand-year-old colonies. Strictly speaking, most settlements were still under construction when the Azal arrived.

Even the oldest terraformed worlds were young enough that their first settlers remained within living memory. Forests had been planted by people whose grandchildren now lived beneath them. Oceans had names older than the water that filled them. Local traditions, accents, and rivalries had formed quickly, but nearly every colony still carried some direct connection to Earth. Countries on Earth existed in their own traditional rights, although inter-Earth conflict had settled down to loud politics and embargoes rather than war. Solar System Colonies were usually cooperative efforts by many different nations, and the fruit of such labor was often shared.

Humanity had spread far.

It had not had time to settle.

### Years 2191–2201: Departure

Humanity’s first permanent settlements beyond Earth expanded from orbital habitats, Luna, Mars, and existing deep-space infrastructure.

The earliest extrasolar missions carried:

- Survey crews.
- Engineers.
- automated construction systems.
- genetic archives.
- reactor components.
- cryogenic colonists.
- terraforming organisms.

These missions were not intended to create independent civilizations, but were meant to establish footholds. Most early colonists expected constant contact with Earth and regular resupply. Few plans accounted for the possibility that a colony might be forced to survive alone.

### Years 2201–2221: The First Footholds

The first extrasolar habitats became permanent settlements.

Most people still lived inside, sealed stations, underground chambers, and pressurized valleys. Terraforming began on several promising worlds, but the work remained experimental. The first generation of colonial xenos appeared during this period. Most were microscopic organisms, fungi, soil-builders, algae, and simple ecological regulator, designed by humans to help lay the foundation of possible future human habitation.

Failures were common.

Several colonies were abandoned before their names became widely known.

### Years 2221–2246: The Sowing

Terraforming technology improved rapidly.

Humanity learned how to introduce complete ecological chains rather than isolated organisms. Plants, fungal systems, animals, and increasingly complex xenos were deployed to regulate developing environments. Terraformers earned much respect during this time, and became the top stars of humanity’s future. The first worlds reached limited outdoor habitability.

A person might walk outside without a sealed suit for minutes or hours, though respirators and weather warnings remained common. This was the period when many of humanity’s most famous colonies were founded.

It was also when increasingly dangerous xeno lines began appearing in restricted research programs, although such knowledge was blocked off from the general public

Years 2246–2261: The Expansion

Colonial growth accelerated. Established worlds exported food, organisms, machinery, and trained personnel to newer projects. Private charters founded specialized settlements around agriculture, mining, research, and manufacturing.

Some colonists were born beneath open skies. Others still lived in sealed habitats only a few systems away.

Human society began developing recognizable regional cultures, but these differences were recent. People from different colonies still watched the same Earth entertainment, used the same technical standards, and often had parents or grandparents born in the Solar System. There were heavy traces of Earth Elitism beginning to rise, and Colonists received less respect than those who were born on the homeworld. Core worlds also began to project such disrespect on the outer worlds.

Earth remained the political and cultural center of humanity, even as frontier colonies increasingly resisted its control.

### Years 2261–2274: The Fracture

The final decade was marked by increasing instability.

Colonial failures became harder to conceal. Restricted biological programs expanded. Military infrastructure appeared in distant systems. Ships and research facilities disappeared from public records.

Then humanity encountered the Azal. The details of the conflict remain fragmented. Some records describe a war.

Others describe an evacuation, containment effort, or series of desperate delaying actions.

By the final battle, humanity had occupied the stars for only a century. Most terraforming projects remained unfinished. Most colonies were not prepared to survive without trade. Many of the organisms designed to support human settlement continued operating after the humans were gone.

## Human Space

Human space was not a unified empire.

It was a loose network of settled systems connected by shipping routes, communication relays, political agreements, and dependence on Earth-based industry.

Colonists usually divided human space into four broad regions.

### The Solar Core

Earth, Luna, Mars, and the major habitats of the original Solar System.

Core citizens often experienced space as something controlled and regulated.

Air, water, gravity, and temperature were artificial in many habitats, but the systems maintaining them were old, redundant, and professionally managed.

To frontier colonists, the Core could seem impossibly safe.

To Core citizens, the frontier often seemed recklessly improvised.

### The Transit Arc

A chain of stations, industrial colonies, fuel depots, and shipyards connecting the Core to the major colony systems.

The Arc existed to keep people and cargo moving.

Its inhabitants lived around:

- Dock schedules.
- Freight seasons.
- Repair contracts.
- Migrant labor.
- Passing crews.
- Emergency diversions.

People born on the Arc often identified more strongly with ships, stations, or professions than with planets. They were accustomed to strangers and skeptical of anyone claiming that a place would remain stable forever.

### The Settled Colonies

The oldest and most successful extrasolar worlds. “Settled” did not mean fully terraformed. It meant that a substantial civilian population could survive there using locally maintained infrastructure and resources.

A settled colony might possess:

- Breathable regions.
- Open agriculture.
- Artificial forests.
- Stable reservoirs.
- Local weather.
- Several cities.

It would still rely on atmospheric processors, engineered organisms, orbital systems, or extensive environmental monitoring. The oldest Settled Colonies had existed for roughly sixty to seventy years. Their cultures were distinct, but young.

### The Active Frontier

New colonies, experimental projects, research systems, mining settlements, and partially developed worlds.

Most frontier residents still lived behind pressure doors.

Their worlds might eventually become habitable, but many inhabitants knew they would not live long enough to see the work completed. Frontier settlements depended heavily on specialists.A single experienced surgeon, reactor engineer, hydrologist, or xenobiologist could be more important to a colony than its official governor.

## Major Colony Origins

The following colonies can serve as character homeworlds. They represent some of the best-known environments and cultures created during humanity’s century among the stars.

Characters do not need to have been born there. They may have worked, studied, served, or taken refuge on one of these worlds.

Statuses for the major colonies are included for characters as of 2274 AD.

## Meridian

Status: Wiped out

### The First Green World

Meridian was the first extrasolar colony to support a large population beneath an open sky. Formerly known as “Proxima Centauri b”, it was never completely Earthlike. Its atmosphere remained thin, its seasons depended on orbital climate systems, and many regions were closed because of unstable soil or dangerous xeno growth.

None of that diminished its symbolic importance. When Meridian held its first public First Breath ceremony, images were transmitted throughout human space. Thousands of settlers stood outside without sealed suits while artificial rain fell over the colony basin.

For many people, Meridian proved that humanity could build a new home.

### The Landscape

Meridian’s inhabited regions consisted of broad temperate valleys surrounded by dry highlands.

Its famous green plains were maintained by:

- Deep-rooted soil organisms.
- Engineered grazing animals.
- Atmospheric fungal colonies.
- Artificial river systems.
- Orbital mirrors controlling seasonal light.

The landscape looked natural from a distance. Up close, nearly every species had been placed there deliberately.

### Life on Meridian

Meridian had cities, farms, universities, and permanent roads. Most residents could spend their entire lives without wearing a pressure suit. Environmental alerts were still part of daily life, although the people were more than equipped to deal with it. Sometimes, large areas closed during ecological correction cycles.

Meridian citizens were proud of living on humanity’s first completed world, though terraformers objected to the word completed.

### Culture

Meridian culture emphasized stewardship.

Residents were taught that the world had been inherited from people who sacrificed their lives building it. Wastefulness was treated as disrespectful, especially when involving water, soil, or living organisms.The people who lived there were a large melting pot of different cultures from Earth, but were all reliant on being hardy and self-sufficient. They regarded people born on Earth as wasteful, and residents of outer-colonies as foolhardy.

Many Meridian families maintained small gardens containing plants descended from the colony’s earliest seed stock.

Common attitudes

A character from Meridian might:

- Consider terraforming humanity’s greatest achievement.
- Feel responsible for protecting engineered ecosystems.
- Underestimate how dangerous younger colonies are.
- Resent the suggestion that their world is artificial.
- Carry guilt over leaving one of humanity’s safest colonies.

Common xenos

Meridian used mostly regulated ecological organisms:

- Soil aerators.
- Pollinators.
- Herding Chase strains.
- Orchard Guards.
- Water-filtering fungal colonies.

Dangerous xenos were officially rare.

Unofficially, several restricted research institutes operated beyond the settled valleys.

## Calder Station

Status: Functioning

### The Shipyard Between Worlds

Calder is not a planet. It is a massive industrial habitat constructed around a resource-rich moon along the Transit Arc. Most ships traveling toward the frontier pass through Calder or carried components manufactured there.

The station grew so quickly that no complete map of it remained accurate for long. New docks, worker habitats, cargo spines, and fabrication chambers were continuously attached to older structures.

### The Environment

Calder’s residents live under artificial gravity inside metal corridors.

The station smells of coolant, machine oil, and recycled air. Many sections have different gravity, power standards, and architectural designs depending on when they are constructed.

Children learn station routes through color markings and machine sounds rather than street names.

### Life on Calder

Calder never sleeps.

Work follows ship arrivals rather than a planetary day. A family might eat breakfast while another shift prepared for bed and a third responded to an incoming emergency vessel. Living space is limited. Tools, food, spare parts, and privacy are all carefully managed. People regularly arrive from distant colonies and disappeared again within days.

### Culture

Calder values usefulness. A person earns respect by being able to repair something, find something, move something, or keep calm when a system fails. Possessions are frequently reused, modified, traded, and inherited.

Throwing away functional equipment is considered nearly immoral. Calder residents have little patience for planetary colonists who treated ships as temporary transportation rather than life-support systems.

Calderians have developed some resentment for core-world residents. After all, they felt used to a gross extent, as a mere transportation system. They also have a harder trouble former closeness with people - families came and went, and most relationships were temporary.

Common attitudes

A character from Calder might:

- Feel comfortable in crowded or noisy environments.
- Sleep better when machinery is audible.
- Keep broken equipment for spare parts.
- Distrust open spaces.
- Think of ships as communities rather than vehicles.
- Have friends or relatives spread throughout human space.

Common xenos

Calder used relatively few large xenos.

Engineered organisms were used for:

- Waste processing.
- Air-quality monitoring.
- Cleaning inaccessible machinery.
- Detecting leaks.
- Breaking down industrial contamination.

Escaped maintenance organisms sometimes established colonies in sealed, abandoned sections of the station.

## Nacre

Status: Alive

### The Ocean Under Construction

Nacre began as a cold world covered in deep ice and mineral-heavy subsurface water. Although hesitant at first, humanity soon came to understand it as a prime terraforming target from work with Europa. Terraforming crews melted selected basins, introduced atmosphere-building organisms, and constructed floating settlements above the new seas.

By the final decades, Nacre possessed the largest artificial ocean in human space. The ocean was less than fifty years old.

### The Landscape

Nacre’s settlements float on linked platforms, artificial islands, and anchored biological structures.

Below them stretch dark water filled with engineered life. The seas regulate planetary temperature, produce atmospheric moisture, and support enormous filtration ecosystems. Storms are frequent and difficult to predict.

Entire settlements are moved when currents change.

### Life on Nacre

Water shapes every part of daily life.

Children learn emergency flotation before they learned to read. Public buildings contain flood compartments. Families track storms, pressure shifts, and plankton blooms. Fresh water and ocean water are treated as completely different resources.

The sea provides food and energy, but remains dangerous. Several organisms released into the deeper basins are no longer regularly monitored.

### Culture

Nacre culture emphasizes adaptability.

Homes, jobs, and even towns moved. Plans change with weather. People expect systems to fail and communities to reorganize around the failure. Nacre colonists are known for communal emergency response. During storms, personal property matter less than maintaining the integrity of the settlement. Everything was shared, and everyone was part of the family.

Common attitudes

A character from Nacre might:

- Remain calm during environmental emergencies.
- Dislike wasting water despite growing up surrounded by it.
- Trust community response more than centralized authority.
- Feel uncomfortable far from a major water source.
- View permanence as suspicious or unrealistic..

Common xenos

Nacre’s ecosystems included:

- Water-filtering colonial organisms.
- Engineered kelp forests.
- Mobile pollinators.
- Guard organisms protecting filtration beds.
- Chase predators regulating fast-breeding aquatic species.
- Endurance organisms designed for high pressure and extreme cold.

Contamination from microscopic marine xenos was a constant concern.

## Orison

Status: Alive, although radio silence since the start of the battle

### The Charter Garden

Orison was founded by an agricultural charter intending to produce food, pharmaceuticals, and biological material for dozens of frontier colonies. An oddity in its own right, Orison was unusually bountiful and took to terraforming quite well.

Its warm climate and engineered atmosphere allowed rapid biological growth. Within twenty years, Orison became one of humanity’s largest exporters of living material. It also became one of the most tightly controlled colonies.

### The Landscape

Orison was covered in vast cultivated regions:

- Fungal fields.
- Engineered orchards.
- Protein marshes.
- medicinal forests.
- seed-production plains.
- enclosed xenogenesis reserves.

The environment was intensely productive but unstable. Growth had to be cut back continuously. Unmanaged land could become impassable within weeks.

### Life on Orison

Nearly everyone’s work related to biological production.

Families might manage one orchard strain for generations, even though those “generations” covered only a few decades. Housing, healthcare, and legal status were often tied to employment contracts. The charter controlled access to seed stock, medicine, and transport.

Residents ate well compared with most frontier colonists, but ownership of the colony’s biological wealth remained concentrated.

### Culture

Orison developed a sharp divide between corporate administrators and field communities.

Administrators saw the colony as a productive system requiring strict management.

Field workers saw themselves as the people who actually understood the living world beneath the reports.

Illegal seed exchanges, unauthorized cultivation, and concealed biological modifications became common forms of resistance. Protests and riots erupted often on the world, and rumors of open revolution spread amongst other colonies from one so self-sufficient.

Common attitudes

A character from Orison might:

- Possess strong opinions about who owns living things.
- Be skilled at recognizing altered crops or xenos.
- Distrust corporate medical programs.
- Treat food as both culture and political power.
- Have participated in smuggling seed stock or medicine.
- Know that dangerous organisms were hidden inside legitimate research programs.

Common xenos

Orison contained the widest public variety of engineered organisms.

These included:

- Agricultural Guards.
- Pest-hunting Chase strains.
- Pollinating Ambush organisms.
- Soil-processing fungal masses.
- Regenerative livestock.
- Chemical-producing plant colonies.

Restricted facilities known informally as black gardens developed more dangerous lines.

## Hestia

Status: Wiped out

### The World That Was Still Becoming

Hestia was a young frontier colony orbiting a dim star. Its surface was bitterly cold, its atmosphere thin, and its usable water trapped beneath deep ice. Terraforming had warmed a chain of equatorial valleys, but the inhabited regions remained separated by hundreds of kilometers of hostile terrain.

Hestia was expected to become broadly habitable within another seventy years. The colonists who began the work knew they would never see it finished.

### The Landscape

Hestia’s settlements were built around geothermal wells, reactor complexes, and atmosphere-processing towers. Outside the warmed valleys, the planet remained frozen and dark.

Artificial forests grew beneath transparent thermal canopies. Fungal networks spread heat through the soil. Endurance xenos maintained equipment in regions too cold for regular human access.

### Life on Hestia

Colonists lived close to the machinery sustaining them. Every resident knew basic emergency procedures. Power failures were community emergencies. Exterior travel required planning, shelter markers, and strict return schedules. The colony had few luxuries but strong local support networks.

People depended on one another because outside help could take months to arrive.

### Culture

Hestians respected endurance, preparation, and contribution

Celebrations focused on environmental milestones:

- A new valley reaching safe temperature.
- The first local harvest.
- The opening of a road.
- A full week without atmospheric alarms.

Hestians were proud of building a world they would never personally enjoy. After all was said and done, people there were said to be kind, resilient, and most of all, enduring.

Common attitudes

A character from Hestia might:

- Prepare excessively for routine travel.
- Judge people by whether they contribute during emergencies.
- Distrust promises of rapid rescue.
- Consider personal comfort less important than collective survival.
- Feel responsible for finishing the work of dead colonists.

Common xenos

Hestia relied heavily on Endurance organisms:

- Heat-carrying fungal networks.
- Cold-resistant excavators.
- Regenerative maintenance animals.
- Ice-processing colonies.
- Guards protecting thermal infrastructure.

Several species remained active after their assigned facilities were abandoned.

## Ilyon

Status: Wiped out

### The Military Colony

Ilyon was officially a logistics and ship-support colony. Its public settlements included training grounds, repair docks, industrial habitats, and housing for military families.

Its restricted sectors contained weapons research, biological testing, and defensive infrastructure intended for a conflict the public did not yet understand. By the final decade, Ilyon became one of the primary staging grounds for humanity’s response to the Azal.

### The Environment

Ilyon’s inhabited moon was only partially terraformed.

Most civilians lived beneath interconnected domes, while military zones extended into underground bunkers and orbital platforms.

The colony’s limited open terrain was used for many vehicle and weapons trials.

### Life on Ilyon

Military and civilian life overlapped constantly.

Children grew up around uniforms, departure ceremonies, and security restrictions. Supply shortages were explained as necessary sacrifices. Certain parts of the colony did not appear on civilian maps.

As the conflict worsened, wounded personnel and displaced colonists arrived from other systems.

Many never left.

### Culture

Ilyon residents valued duty, competence, and emotional control.

They were accustomed to missing information. Orders were often followed before explanations were provided, if explanations ever came. Some residents remained fiercely loyal to the institutions that had protected humanity.

Others believed those same institutions had concealed the truth until it was too late.

Common attitudes

A character from Ilyon might:

- Follow emergency procedures automatically.
- Feel responsible for civilians.
- Distrust classified authority.
- Know more about the final conflict than they admit.
- Have lost family members before the official war began.
- Believe that dangerous xeno research was justified by necessity.

Common xenos

Ilyon’s public terraforming organisms were limited.

Restricted facilities studied:

- Combat-adapted Chase strains.
- Armored Guard organisms.
- Extreme-regeneration Endurance lines.
- Human-compatible biological augmentation.
- Xenos capable of operating in vacuum or damaged ships.

Many records were destroyed during the final evacuation.

Life Between Colonies

Most characters were not defined by one world alone.

A person might have been born on Meridian, educated on Calder, and then contracted to Orison.

Travel between colonies could take long enough that people treated departure seriously.

Messages arrived late. Political situations changed during transit. Children returned to worlds their parents no longer recognized. Ships became temporary societies. A person’s shipboard behavior often mattered more than their colony of origin.

## Choosing a Character Origin

A player can create a simple origin by choosing three details. This is meant as a simple guide, and can be changed or altered depending on what the given player desires.

### 1. Home

Choose a colony, station, ship, frontier settlement, or make your own.

What did the character see when they looked outside?

### 2. Generation

Choose one:

- Earthborn: Born on Earth before leaving human space’s center.
- Transit-born: Born aboard a ship or station.
- Domeborn: Born in a sealed colony.
- Open-sky: Born after a colony became partially habitable.
- Frontier-born: Born where terraforming remained incomplete.

Because only one century passed, Earthborn characters need not be elderly. Many adults may have been born in the Solar Core and relocated as children.

### 3. Work

What system, community, or project depended on the character? Their official Background gives their broad role. Their profession gives it personal detail.

## Character Questions

Each player could answer several of these questions.

### Home

- Was the outside safe?
- What color was the sky?
- What resource was always scarce?
- What sound meant the habitat was functioning?
- What local food do you still miss?

### Terraforming

- What part of your world was created during your lifetime?
- What organism was common around your settlement?
- What environmental warning did everyone recognize?
- Which terraforming profession did your community respect most?
- Did you believe the colony would ever be completed?

### Society

- Who controlled the settlement?
- Were you a citizen, employee, contractor, soldier, or dependent?
- Which rule kept people alive?
- Which rule existed only to maintain authority?
- What did people from other colonies misunderstand about your home?

### Motivation

- What did you do during the first evacuation?
- Who did you expect to meet again?
- What order did you refuse—or obey?
- What part of old human space do you still believe can be restored?
- Do you think humanity has lost? Or is there still hope upon returning home?
